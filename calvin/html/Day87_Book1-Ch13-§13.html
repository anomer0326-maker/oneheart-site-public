Day 87 — Book 1, Chapter 13, §13

0 — Orientation (1 minute) –

Yesterday I told you to write two words across the top of the page: ministry and inherent power. Open today's section and look at the second sentence. they dispensed the gifts of God **as his ministers**, whereas he exerted **his own inherent might**. ⚠️⚠️⚠️ The same two words. One section later. Word for word. §12 S9 said not the **ministry** of forgiving sins, but the **inherent power** which the Lord declares he will not give to another; §13 S2 says as his **ministers** … his own **inherent** might. §12 and §13 are not two arguments. They are one argument with a section break in the middle, and Calvin has re-banked the distinction rather than restating it. If a test asks you what §13 adds, the answer is not a new premise — it is a new field of application: yesterday the distinction governed forgiveness, today it governs miracles.

Now the opening, and it is not what you expect. §11 opened with a thesis. §12 opened with a thesis (The divinity of Christ, if judged by the works …, becomes still more evident). §13 opens with a shout. **How clearly and transparently does this appear in his miracles?** ⚠️⚠️ Three things at once. (i) It is an exclamatory inversion wearing a question mark — How + adverb + does + S + V — and it is not asking you anything. (ii) **this** is an anaphor with no noun in sight: its antecedent is across the section break, in §12 — the divinity of Christ, or more precisely the proof from what he does. Calvin has started mid-sentence, grammatically speaking. (iii) The comparative ladder from §12 S1 (still more evident) has just been climbed one more rung: evident → still more evident → **clearly and transparently**. Three sections, three grades of brightness. Mark it, because it is a chapter-map disguised as adverbs.

And here is the move that makes §13 a much better argument than a careless reader will think it is. Calvin gives the whole case away in his second sentence and wins anyway. **I admit** that similar and equal miracles were performed by the prophets and apostles. ⚠️⚠️⚠️ Stop. Read that again. The prophets parted a sea; Elijah raised a widow's son; Peter's shadow healed. Calvin does not minimise a single one of them — he calls them **similar and equal**. He has just conceded that the miracles themselves prove nothing. ⚠️ If a test option says Calvin argues Christ's miracles were greater, or more numerous, or of a kind the prophets could not perform, it has read the section backwards. The argument does not live in what was done. It lives in **this very essential difference** — in how, and on whose power. Underline the word difference and write beside it: NOT in the works, in the MODE.

Then watch him handle the one piece of evidence that could sink him, and watch how fast. **Sometimes, indeed,** he used prayer, that he might ascribe glory to the Father, **but** we see that **for the most part** his own proper power is displayed. (S3) ⚠️⚠️ That is a counter-example, and Calvin raises it himself. Christ at the tomb of Lazarus looks exactly like Elijah at Zarephath — a man asking God. A weaker writer suppresses this. Calvin concedes it in six words (Sometimes, indeed, he used prayer), explains it in nine (that he might ascribe glory to the Father), and turns it in thirteen. ⚠️ Note the two hedges that make the turn honest: **for the most part** — not always; is **displayed** — a matter of what the record shows. Calvin is arguing from a preponderance, not from a universal, and he says so. An option that has him claiming Christ never prayed is not a misreading of the argument; it is a misreading of an adverb.

The spine of the second half is a single logical form, and you should be able to name it. And **how should not he be** the true author of miracles, **who, of his own authority, commissions others to perform them?** (S4) ⚠️⚠️ That is an a fortiori from delegation: the one who hands out a power must possess it, and possess it more fully than those he hands it to. Then the evidence in two stages — what Christ gave (he gave power to the apostles to cast out devils, cure the lepers, raise the dead, &c., S5, the Evangelist's testimony) and what the apostles admitted (their whole power was **derived from** Christ, S6, their own testimony, given not in words but by the mode in which they performed this ministry). And then the proof text, and it is chosen with great care: **“In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth,”** says Peter (Acts 3:6), **“rise up and walk.”** ⚠️⚠️⚠️ Six words of quotation and the entire argument is in the first four. Peter does not say rise up and walk. He says in whose name he says it. The borrowed name is the confession of derived power. Christ, at the same tomb-side and bed-side, says Talitha cumi and nothing else.

Today's 3 Big Points

  1. Interrogative shapes that are not asking anything — two of them, built differently, and both testable. ① **How clearly and transparently does this appear** in his miracles? (S1) — exclamation in interrogative dress: How + adverb(s) + do/does + S + V, with do-support and subject–auxiliary inversion because How + adverb has been fronted. ⚠️ Modern English writes this with a full stop (How clearly this appears in his miracles!) and no inversion; Beveridge's period keeps the inversion and the question mark. ② And **how should not he be** the true author of miracles …? (S4) — a rhetorical modal, expecting the answer he must be, and note the period negative placement: should **not he** be, where modern English writes should he not be. ⚠️⚠️ The negative sits before the subject pronoun. That is not an error and it is the most likely grammar item in the section. Formula 1.
  2. The concession-reversal frame, run three times in three different builds. ① **I admit that** A; **but** there is this very essential difference, **that** B (S2) — concession, adversative, and then a cataphoric this … that apposition (this very essential difference is empty until the that-clause fills it). ② **whereas** (S2) — a subordinating contrastive conjunction, no inversion, no comma-splice; it does not mean while in time. ③ **Sometimes, indeed,** A, **but** B (S3) — the concessive indeed (= it is true), then the reversal, hedged by for the most part. ⚠️ Three concessions in two sentences. Calvin's habit: give the opponent his best material first, then show it does not reach the point at issue. Formula 2.
  3. Every preposition of source in this section is doing argumentative work — read the bys, the ofs and the froms as a single system. performed **by** the prophets and apostles (S2) · dispensed the gifts **of God** (S2) · **of** his own authority (S4) · their whole power was derived **from** Christ (S6) · **In the name of** Jesus Christ of Nazareth (S7) · performed **by his own energy** (S8). ⚠️⚠️ Line them up and the section reads itself: every agent in it either borrows power or owns it, and the preposition tells you which. And notice the pair that frames the whole: **performed by** the prophets and apostles (S2, human instruments) ↔ **performed by his own energy** (S8, the source itself). Same verb, same voice, different kind of by. Formula 3.

1 — Full Text (⚠️ invariable) — 8 sentences –

13. How clearly and transparently does this appear in his miracles? I admit that similar and equal miracles were performed by the prophets and apostles; but there is this very essential difference, that they dispensed the gifts of God as his ministers, whereas he exerted his own inherent might. Sometimes, indeed, he used prayer, that he might ascribe glory to the Father, but we see that for the most part his own proper power is displayed. And how should not he be the true author of miracles, who, of his own authority, commissions others to perform them? For the Evangelist relates that he gave power to the apostles to cast out devils, cure the lepers, raise the dead, &c. And they, by the mode in which they performed this ministry, showed plainly that their whole power was derived from Christ. “In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth,” says Peter (Acts 3:6), “rise up and walk.” It is not surprising, then, that Christ appealed to his miracles in order to subdue the unbelief of the Jews, inasmuch as these were performed by his own energy, and therefore bore the most ample testimony to his divinity.

Sentence count: 8 (S1–S8). One paragraph, three movements: the shout and the concession that founds the whole argument — How clearly and transparently …? followed at once by I admit that similar and equal miracles were performed by the prophets and apostles, and then the pivot, but there is this very essential difference — ministers dispensing versus his own inherent might — with the one awkward counter-example (Sometimes, indeed, he used prayer) raised and answered in the same breath (S1–S3) · the a fortiori from delegation and its two witnesses — the Evangelist on what Christ gave (he gave power to the apostles to cast out devils, cure the lepers, raise the dead, &c.) and the apostles on what they had received (their whole power was derived from Christ), sealed by Peter's borrowed name (S4–S7) · the conclusion, which is about evidence rather than about power — why Christ could rest his case on the miracles at all: these were performed by his own energy, and therefore bore the most ample testimony to his divinity (S8).

Text note (not a correction to the quotation). Five features of the printed page, all left exactly as they stand. (i) ⚠️⚠️ A page break falls inside S2, and inside a word. The file prints but there is this very es- 121 / (blank line) / sential difference — the running number 121 sits in the middle of the sentence, and the word essential is split by a line-break hyphen across the seam. Joined; the number removed. Nothing else touched. (ii) Two more line-break hyphens in S8, both in the last two lines of the section: inas- / much as → inasmuch as, and testi- / mony → testimony. These are typesetting hyphens, not the edition's spelling; joined. (iii) ⚠️ &c. is printed, not etc. — the older ampersand form of et cetera, and it is the only abbreviation of its kind in the section. Left as printed. (Note what it implies: the list in S5 is explicitly open. Calvin is summarising a commission, not itemising it.) (iv) The Acts 3:6 quotation is split around the speech tag — “In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth,” says Peter (Acts 3:6), “rise up and walk.” — with curly quotation marks, the reference inside the interrupting clause rather than after the quotation, and the comma before the closing quotation mark in the first half. ⚠️ Compare §12's ,” (Is. 43:25) pattern, where the reference followed the completed quotation. The edition is not consistent about this, and the inconsistency is kept. (v) Typography kept: the question mark on the exclamatory S1; the semicolon before but in S2 (heavier than a comma — it marks the pivot); the commas isolating indeed in S3 and then in S8; the double commas around the interposed of his own authority in S4 and around by the mode in which they performed this ministry in S6; and the non-restrictive comma before who in S4.

Examiner's Eye. Five traps, and the first one is the whole section.

  • Trap 1 — the difference is in the mode, never in the miracles (S2). ⚠️⚠️⚠️ I admit that **similar and equal** miracles were performed by the prophets and apostles; **but there is this very essential difference, that** they dispensed the gifts of God **as his ministers**, whereas he exerted **his own inherent might**. similar and equal is a full concession and it is not grudging. An option that says Christ's miracles were greater, more numerous, unprecedented, or of a kind no prophet could work has inverted the section's opening concession — and it has also destroyed the argument, because if the works themselves differed, Calvin would not need the distinction at all. The whole weight rests on as his ministers versus his own inherent might, and on nothing else.
  • Trap 2 — Sometimes, indeed, he used prayer is conceded, not denied (S3). ⚠️⚠️ Calvin admits the prayers, gives a reason (that he might ascribe glory to the Father), and then outweighs them (**for the most part** his own proper power is displayed). An option that has Calvin denying that Christ prayed, or claiming that Christ never invoked the Father when working miracles, has hardened a preponderance into a universal. ⚠️ Equally wrong in the other direction: an option that treats the prayers as evidence against Christ's divinity in Calvin's own eyes. He does not think they tell against it; he thinks they have a different purpose — glory ascribed, not power borrowed.
  • Trap 3 — who is the author and who is the minister (S4–S6). ⚠️⚠️ how should not he be the **true author** of miracles, who, **of his own authority**, commissions others to perform them? and their **whole** power was **derived from** Christ. The apostles are the derived party; Christ is the source. An option that has the apostles sharing in an inherent power, or that reads he gave power to the apostles (S5) as evidence that the power was Christ's to pass on in the sense of parting with it, has crossed the section's two columns. ⚠️ Note **whole** in S6 — their power, in its entirety, came from him: no remainder, no native residue. The word is there to close exactly that loophole.
  • Trap 4 — what S8 actually concludes. ⚠️ It is not surprising, then, that Christ **appealed to his miracles** in order to **subdue the unbelief** of the Jews. The conclusion of §13 is about the legitimacy of an appeal to evidence, not about the raw fact of power. Calvin is explaining why Christ could rest an argument on his miracles — because being worked by his own energy they bore the most ample **testimony** to what he was. An option that reads S8 as "miracles prove divinity" has skipped the qualifier inasmuch as these were performed by his own energy, which is the entire condition. Miracles as such prove nothing; miracles worked by inherent energy testify.
  • Trap 5 — the antecedent of this in S1, and the grade of the adverbs. ⚠️ How clearly and transparently does **this** appear in his miracles? — this reaches back across the section break to §12, to the divinity proved from works. An option that makes this refer to the miracles themselves has made the sentence say nothing (how clearly do the miracles appear in his miracles). ⚠️ And the adverbs: clearly and transparently continues §12's still more evident; they do not announce that this is the final or strongest proof — §14 will start again with Again, …. A comparative sequence is not a ranking.

4 — Today's Grammar Formulas –

Formula 1 — Interrogative forms that assert: the exclamative How, and the rhetorical should not he

   ① EXCLAMATIVE-INTERROGATIVE
      How + ADVERB(S) + do/does/did + S + V ... ?
      ⚠ THREE rules fire at once: fronting → inversion → do-support
      ⚠ FORM = question · FORCE = exclamation · ANSWER expected = none
      ⚠ modern English:  How clearly this appears ...!   (no inversion, "!" not "?")

      S1  How clearly and transparently DOES THIS APPEAR in his miracles?
            = This appears very clearly and transparently in his miracles.

   ② RHETORICAL MODAL, with PERIOD NEGATIVE PLACEMENT
      how should NOT + PRONOUN-SUBJECT + BE ... ?      ← period order
      how should + PRONOUN-SUBJECT + not + BE ... ?    ← modern order
      ⚠ "how ... not ...?" ⇒ STRONG AFFIRMATION ("he certainly is")

      S4  And how SHOULD NOT HE BE the true author of miracles, who ... ?
            = He must certainly be the true author of miracles.

⚠️ Trap 1 — "correcting" should not he be. It is correct as printed. When the subject is a pronoun, modern English moves not after it (should he not be); period English does not have to. With a full noun subject even modern English keeps the older order (Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?). ⚠️ Trap 2 — losing the inversion in ①. How clearly **this appears**? is ungrammatical as a question and mispunctuated as an exclamation. Once How + adverb is fronted in an interrogative, the auxiliary must come before the subject, and a lexical verb must borrow do. ⚠️ Trap 3 — translating either as a real question. Neither expects an answer. ⚠️ Trap 4 — the double negative in ②. how + not does not cancel to a denial; it produces emphatic affirmation.

Transformation drill. 그의 권능이 그의 기적들 안에서 얼마나 환히 드러나는가! 그리고 다른 이들에게 권한을 주는 그가 어찌 그 권능의 참된 근원이 아닐 수 있겠는가? → How clearly and transparently does his power appear in his miracles! And how should not he be the true source of that power, who, of his own authority, commissions others? ⚠️ Check three things: the do-support (does his power appear, never appears his power); the negative before the pronoun in the second sentence; and that neither sentence is asking anything.

Formula 2 — The concession–reversal frame, three builds, and the cataphoric this … that

   ① I ADMIT that A ;  BUT  B                        (S2)   ← full concession, semicolon pivot
   ② SOMETIMES, INDEED, A ,  BUT  B                  (S3)   ← "indeed" = it is true (concessive)
   ③ A ,  WHEREAS  B                                 (S2)   ← SUBORDINATING contrast
        ⚠ whereas = "while by contrast" — NO inversion, NOT temporal, NOT the legal "whereas"

   + the cataphoric apposition that carries the pivot:
      there is THIS very essential difference,  THAT  ...   (S2)
      ⚠ "this" points FORWARD · the comma = a colon · "that" = APPOSITIVE, not relative
      ⚠ TEST: can you insert "namely"?  yes → appositive.  can you swap in "which"?  no.

   + the honest hedges that keep a concession from becoming a universal:
      FOR THE MOST PART ...  /  ... IS DISPLAYED      (S3)

⚠️ Trap 1 — treating the concession as grudging or partial. similar and **equal** is unqualified, and the argument needs it to be. ⚠️ Trap 2 — inverting after whereas. whereas **he exerted**, never whereas exerted he; whereas is subordinating, so normal order. ⚠️ Trap 3 — misparsing that after difference. Appositive, not relative: it supplies the difference rather than describing it. ⚠️ Trap 4 — dropping the hedge. for the most part ≠ always. A universalised Calvin is a misquoted Calvin. ⚠️ Trap 5 — the emphatic indeed. Sometimes, indeed, …, but … is concessive (it is true that), not intensifying (very much so). The following but is your tell.

Transformation drill. 나는 선지자들도 동등한 기적을 행했음을 인정한다. 그러나 바로 이 본질적인 차이가 있으니, 곧 그들은 종으로서 하나님의 선물을 나누어 준 반면, 그는 자기 자신의 고유한 권능을 발휘하셨다는 것이다. 물론 그가 때로는 기도하셨다. 그러나 대부분의 경우 드러나는 것은 그 자신의 권능이다. → I admit that the prophets also performed equal miracles; but there is this very essential difference, that they dispensed the gifts of God as his ministers, whereas he exerted his own inherent might. Sometimes, indeed, he used prayer; but for the most part his own proper power is displayed. ⚠️ Four checks: the semicolon before but; this … that with the comma doing a colon's job; no inversion after whereas; and the hedge for the most part left in.

   BY + PERSON        "performed BY the prophets and apostles"     (S2)  ← human instrument
   BY + POWER         "performed BY HIS OWN ENERGY"                (S8)  ← the source itself

The construction is identical and the argument depends on their being different. ⚠️ Trap 2 — reading as his ministers as comparison. as = in the capacity of, not like. ⚠️ Trap 3 — losing own / whole. his **own** inherent might, his **own** proper power, **of his own** authority, by his **own** energy; their **whole** power. 6 — Background in 5 Minutes –

1. Where §13 sits, and why it exists at all. Proof three of the series §11 announced (the **first** thing deserving of special observation): §11 prophecy applied → §12 works ascribed → §13 power exercised. ⚠️⚠️ Each section closes a door the previous one left ajar, and §13 closes the last one. After §11: the titles may be honorific. After §12: the works may have been done by God through him, as through a prophet. §13 answers exactly that reply — they dispensed the gifts of God **as his ministers**, **whereas** he exerted **his own inherent might**. ⚠️ And the answer is built out of §12's own vocabulary (ministry/ministers, inherent power/inherent might). If a test asks what §13 contributes: not a new premise, but the application of §12's distinction to the one field where the opponent's reply had most force. Then §14 starts a new run — Again, … — from salvation, life and righteousness.

2. The argument's real problem, and Calvin knows it. ⚠️⚠️⚠️ Miracles are a notoriously weak proof, and Scripture itself says so. If there arise among you a prophet … and giveth thee a sign or a wonder, and the sign or the wonder come to pass … thou shalt not hearken (Deut 13:1–3); there shall arise false Christs, and false prophets, and shall shew great signs and wonders (Matt 24:24); whose coming is after the working of Satan with all power and signs and lying wonders (2 Thess 2:9). ⚠️ Calvin is emphatic about this elsewhere. In the Prefatory Address to Francis I (1536, kept in all editions) he refuses to let Rome authenticate its doctrine by miracles, and insists that miracles confirm a doctrine already established from the Word rather than establishing it. So §13 cannot be — and is not — the argument "he worked miracles, therefore he is God." It is the argument "he worked them by his own might, therefore he is God," and the concession in S2 (similar and equal) is precisely Calvin conceding the weak form in order to run the strong one. ⚠️ Guardrail, and it is the most important one today: if you write that Calvin proves Christ's divinity from miracles, you have said something Calvin explicitly refuses to say. He proves it from the mode of the miracles.

3. The distinction's patristic pedigree: per versus a, minister versus author. The contrast between working as an instrument and working from oneself is the standard fourth-century weapon. ⚠️ Athanasius (Orations against the Arians) presses it from creation: the Son is not an instrument God used but the one through whom and in whom all things were made, without himself being made. Cyril of Alexandria repeatedly notes that Christ heals αὐθεντικῶς — on his own warrant — where prophets pray. Augustine gives the grammar of it: the Father works a quo (from whom), the Son per quem (through whom), and yet the Son's working is not instrumental because the per is internal to the one divine operation (De Trinitate I). ⚠️⚠️ Guardrail: Calvin names none of them in §13. §10 mentioned the orthodox doctors of the Church; §§11–13 name nobody. Do not write that Calvin cites Athanasius or Cyril here. He runs a patristic argument in purely biblical dress — which is the method of the Institutes in one sentence, and the third time this week I have had to say it.

4. The a fortiori from delegation, and its limits. S4's move — the one who commissions others must himself be the author — is a form the schoolmen state as an axiom: whatever causes a quality in another possesses that quality more eminently (propter quod unumquodque tale, et illud magis tale). ⚠️ Calvin does not cite it, and you should not claim he does; he simply runs it, and he runs it in the form a lawyer would use rather than a metaphysician: of his own authority is the phrase that carries it. ⚠️⚠️ Where the argument is vulnerable, and it is worth knowing: a plenipotentiary can delegate powers he himself holds only by delegation — an ambassador may sub-commission. Calvin's guard against that objection is the four-word phrase, and only that phrase. This is why S4's of his own authority is the section's real hinge and not a stylistic flourish, and why I made you underline it.

5. The prayers of Christ — a standing problem, answered here in nine words. ⚠️ S3's counter-example is real and old. Arius used it; every subordinationist since has used it; John 11:41–42 is the sharpest case, because Christ prays at Lazarus' tomb and then says out loud why: Father, I thank thee that thou hast heard me. And I knew that thou hearest me always: but **because of the people which stand by I said it**, that they may believe that thou hast sent me. ⚠️⚠️ That verse is Calvin's whole answer in S3, and he does not quote it — that he might **ascribe glory to the Father** is John 11:42 compressed. The prayer had an audience, and the audience was the point. ⚠️ Calvin's fuller treatment belongs elsewhere: the prayers of Christ are the acts of the Mediator, discharged in the office he assumed (Book 2, chs. 12–17), and Calvin's doctrine of accommodation governs how such passages are read. ⚠️⚠️ Guardrail: §13 gives this one clause and moves on. Do not import Book 2 into it, and above all do not write that §13 offers a doctrine of the two natures. It offers a preponderance argument about a narrative pattern.

6. The apostles' own disclaimers — the evidence behind S6 that Calvin never quotes. S6 says the apostles by the **mode** in which they performed this ministry, showed plainly that their whole power was derived from Christ. ⚠️⚠️ That is a summary of a pattern running through Acts, and it is startlingly consistent. Peter at the Beautiful Gate: In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth (3:6) — quoted in S7 — and then, when the crowd stares, why look ye so earnestly on us, **as though by our own power or holiness** we had made this man to walk? (3:12). Peter to Aeneas: **Jesus Christ** maketh thee whole (9:34) — note the subject of the verb. Paul and Barnabas at Lystra, tearing their clothes: we also are men of like passions with you (14:14–15). ⚠️⚠️⚠️ And the negative proof: the seven sons of Sceva, who tried to use the formula without the relation — We adjure you **by Jesus whom Paul preacheth** (19:13) — and were beaten for it. The name works only for those actually commissioned. That is the mode Calvin means. Set this beside Christ: Talitha cumi (Mark 5:41), Young man, I say unto thee, Arise (Luke 7:14), I will; be thou clean (Matt 8:3), Peace, be still (Mark 4:39). Not one invocation. Not one name above his own.

7. The prophets prayed too — which is the other half of S2, and also unquoted. ⚠️ Elijah at Carmel: **Hear me, O LORD, hear me**, that this people may know that thou art the LORD God (1 Kgs 18:36–37). Elijah with the widow's son: he cried unto the LORD (1 Kgs 17:21). Elisha with the Shunammite's son: he … **prayed unto the LORD** (2 Kgs 4:33). Moses: **the LORD** said unto Moses, Lift thou up thy rod (Exod 14:16) — a rod raised on orders. ⚠️⚠️ Every great Old Testament miracle-worker is shown asking or obeying. Calvin does not list these because the pattern is so uniform that his sixteenth-century reader supplies it instantly. But it is the empirical basis of they dispensed the gifts of God as his ministers, and if you want to know whether S2's claim is fair to the text, this is where you check.

8. Modern reception, honestly. ⚠️ The observation that Jesus in the Synoptics heals and exorcises on his own authority, without invocation, adjuration or prayer-formula, in marked contrast to contemporary Jewish and Hellenistic practitioners, is not a confessional claim — it is a commonplace of historical-Jesus scholarship (compare the adjuration formulae in the magical papyri and in Josephus' account of Eleazar, Ant. 8.46–49, with Mark's bare imperatives). Geza Vermes, no friend of Nicene conclusions, notes the absence of the usual apparatus. ⚠️⚠️ Guardrail, and it is the same one as the last two days: the convergence is on the datum, not on the inference. That Jesus is portrayed as commanding rather than petitioning — widely granted. That this portrayal entails consubstantiality with the Father — precisely the disputed step, and no historian's finding settles it. Do not write that modern scholarship vindicates §13. Write that modern scholarship confirms the pattern Calvin builds on, and disputes what follows from it.

9. What §13 deliberately does not do. ⚠️ It says nothing about whether miracles continue, nothing about the apostles' successors, nothing about how the power was conveyed, and nothing about the relation of the natures in the working of a miracle. S3's Sometimes, indeed, he used prayer is the only place the incarnate condition surfaces at all, and it is disposed of in one clause. ⚠️ Guardrail: §13 is a proof of divinity from the mode of working, and that is the whole of it. An answer that has Calvin teaching about the cessation of miracles, or about sacramental power, or about the communicatio idiomatum, has imported three other books into eight sentences.

8 — Exam Problems –

① 어법 (Grammar) — one of the four underlined items is wrong

How clearly and transparently ⓐdoes this appear in his miracles? I admit that similar and equal miracles ⓑwere performed by the prophets and apostles; but there is this very essential difference, that they dispensed the gifts of God as his ministers, ⓒwhereas exerted he his own inherent might. And how ⓓshould not he be the true author of miracles, who, of his own authority, commissions others to perform them?

Answer: ⓒ.

출제 의도. ⓒ inverts after whereas. ⚠️⚠️ whereas is a subordinating conjunction of contrast, and subordinate clauses take normal subject–verb order: whereas **he exerted** his own inherent might (Formula 2 ③). Inversion in English is triggered by fronted negatives (Nor does he …, §12 S6) and by fronted interrogative or exclamative operators (How … does this appear, S1) — not by a subordinator. whereas exerted he is the one item of the four that is actually ungrammatical, in any century.

⚠️ The other three all look suspicious and are all correct. ⓐ **does this appear** — do-support plus subject–auxiliary inversion is obligatory once How + adverb is fronted in an interrogative frame; "correcting" it to this appears would leave a question mark on an uninverted clause. Note the period feature: the force is exclamatory even though the form and punctuation are interrogative — but that is a matter of force, not of grammar, so it is not the error. ⓑ **were performed** — passive with the agent stated (by the prophets and apostles), plural subject miracles, perfectly regular; a reader who feels that Calvin ought to be denying this will suspect the grammar because he dislikes the content. Do not confuse a theological objection with a grammatical one. ⓓ **should not he be** — ⚠️⚠️⚠️ correct as printed, and this is the real trap of the item. Modern English moves not after a pronoun subject (should he not be), but period English places the negative before the subject pronoun in an inverted interrogative; with a noun subject even modern English keeps the older order (Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?). Three of the four underlines test whether you will "fix" good period English. Only one is broken.

② 내용일치 (Comprehension) — which of the following best represents Calvin's argument in §13?

  1. Calvin argues that Christ's miracles were greater in kind and number than those of the prophets and apostles, and that this superiority establishes his divinity.
  2. Calvin grants that the prophets and apostles performed miracles equal to Christ's, and locates the proof of his divinity not in the works themselves but in the manner of working — they distributed God's gifts as his servants, while he exerted a power inherent in himself, and he could confer the power to work miracles on others by his own authority.
  3. Calvin observes that Christ, unlike the prophets, never prayed when performing miracles, and concludes from this uninterrupted independence that the power at work in him was his own.
  4. Calvin argues that because the Jews were convinced by Christ's miracles and abandoned their unbelief, the miracles must have been performed by a power greater than any creature possesses.

Answer: 2.

출제 의도. Option 2 states the section's spine in order: the concession (S2a), the pivot to mode (S2b), and the a fortiori from delegation (S4–S7).

  • 1 — the opening concession inverted, and the commonest error on this section. ⚠️⚠️⚠️ S2 says I admit that **similar and equal** miracles were performed by the prophets and apostles — unqualified, and structurally necessary. Number is never mentioned anywhere in §13. And the option destroys the argument as well as the text: if the works themselves differed, Calvin would not need this very essential difference at all. ⚠️ Note why this distractor is so tempting: it is what most readers assume a defence of Christ's divinity from miracles must say. Calvin refuses the obvious argument because Deut 13:1–3 and Matt 24:24 have already broken it.
  • 3 — a hedge hardened into a universal. ⚠️⚠️ S3 says **Sometimes, indeed, he used prayer**, that he might ascribe glory to the Father, but we see that **for the most part** his own proper power is displayed. Calvin explicitly concedes the prayers and explicitly limits his claim to a preponderance. never prayed and uninterrupted are both stronger than the text. ⚠️ This is a one-adverb trap: everything else in the option is defensible, and for the most part is the only thing standing between it and the right answer. Read the hedges.
  • 4 — purpose read as result, plus a premise the section does not use. ⚠️ S8 says Christ appealed to his miracles **in order to** subdue the unbelief of the Jews — an infinitive of purpose. Calvin reports what the appeal was for, not that it succeeded, and §12 has just shown the Jews trying to kill him. ⚠️ And a power greater than any creature possesses is a degree claim; §13's claim is about origin, not degree — by his own energy, not by a larger energy. Two errors, and the second is the more serious: degree arguments are exactly what S2's concession rules out.

③ 영작 (Composition) — apply today's formulas

그의 권능이 그의 사역 안에서 얼마나 환히, 얼마나 투명하게 드러나는가! 나는 그의 종들도 동등한 일들을 행했음을 인정한다. 그러나 바로 이 본질적인 차이가 있으니, 곧 그들은 주인의 선물을 청지기로서 나누어 준 반면, 그는 자기 자신의 고유한 권능을 발휘하셨다는 것이다. 물론 그가 때로는 기도하셨으나, 대부분의 경우 드러나는 것은 그 자신의 권능이다. 그리고 자기 자신의 권위로 다른 이들에게 그 일을 위임하는 그가, 어찌 그 일의 참된 근원이 아닐 수 있겠는가? 그들의 모든 권능은 그에게서 온 것이었고, 그러므로 그들은 그의 이름으로 말했다.

Target (each formula used, marked):

How clearly and how transparently does his power appear in his work! (Formula 1 ① — How + adverb fronted, do-support, inversion; note the second how repeated for the doublet.) I admit that his servants also performed equal works; but there is this very essential difference, that they dispensed their master's gifts as stewards, whereas he exerted his own inherent might. (Formula 2 ① — I admit that …; but … with the semicolon pivot; the cataphoric this … that; and Formula 2 ③ — whereas with NO inversion.) Sometimes, indeed, he used prayer; but for the most part his own proper power is displayed. (Formula 2 ② — concessive indeed answered by but, and the hedge for the most part kept.) And how should not he be the true author of that work, who, of his own authority, commissions others to perform it? (Formula 1 ② — rhetorical modal with the period negative placement should not he.) Their whole power was derived from him, and therefore they spoke in his name. (Formula 3 — derived from + in the name of, two source markers; and whole kept, closing the loophole.)

출제 의도. Five sentences, five things most likely to break. (i) Do-support and inversion after a fronted How + adverb — the commonest failure is How clearly his power appears?, which is neither a good question nor a good exclamation. (ii) No inversion after whereas — the trap is contamination from the inverted sentence two lines above. (iii) The appositive that after difference — writing the difference which they dispensed makes a relative out of an apposition. (iv) Negative placement in should not he be — period order is required by the drill, and students who "correct" it to should he not be have produced good modern English and failed the exercise. (v) ⚠️ Keeping the hedges and the modifiers. for the most part, his **own** proper, their **whole** power — every one of them is load-bearing, and dropping any of them changes the claim. The single commonest error across the whole exercise is writing equal works in sentence two and then, in the same paragraph, implying his were greater. Calvin never does. Neither may you.

9 — One-Line Wrap-up + Homework –

One-line summary. Proof three of three arrives without a thesis sentence, as a shout with a pronoun pointing back over the section break — **How clearly and transparently does this appear in his miracles?** (S1) — and then Calvin does the one thing that makes the argument work: he gives the whole case away. **I admit that similar and equal miracles were performed by the prophets and apostles** (S2a) — conceded without qualification, because the proof was never going to live in the works; it lives in **this very essential difference, that** they dispensed the gifts of God **as his ministers**, **whereas** he exerted **his own inherent might** (S2b), which is §12 S9's not the ministry … but the inherent power redeployed word for word onto a new field. The awkward evidence is raised by Calvin himself and hedged honestly — **Sometimes, indeed,** he used prayer, that he might ascribe glory to the Father, but … **for the most part** his own **proper** power is displayed (S3) — and then the a fortiori: And **how should not he be the true author** of miracles, **who, of his own authority, commissions others** to perform them? (S4), with two witnesses, the Evangelist on what Christ handed over (he gave power to the apostles to cast out devils, cure the lepers, raise the dead, &c., S5) and the apostles on what they had received — testimony given by manner, not by statement: **by the mode in which** they performed this ministry, showed plainly that their **whole** power was **derived from** Christ (S6), sealed by six words in which the borrowed name comes before the command, **In the name of** Jesus Christ of Nazareth … rise up and walk (S7). And the close is about evidence, not power: It is not surprising, then, that Christ **appealed to** his miracles in order to **subdue the unbelief** of the Jews, **inasmuch as** these were performed **by his own energy**, and therefore bore **the most ample testimony** to his divinity (S8). Grammar: ① interrogatives that assert — How + adverb + does + S + V ? with fronting, inversion and do-support, force exclamatory (S1), and the rhetorical how **should not he** be …? with the period negative before the pronoun subject (S4); ② the concession–reversal frame in three builds — I admit that A; but B with a semicolon pivot and a cataphoric this … that apposition, the subordinating whereas that takes no inversion, and Sometimes, **indeed**, A, but B with the hedges for the most part and is displayed left standing (S2–S3); ③ source markers as a system — by + persons (S2) versus by + his own energy (S8), of his own authority (S4), derived from (S6), in the name of (S7), as his ministers (S2), all serving the one pair inherent (in- + haerēre, sticks fast in) ↔ derived (dē- + rīvus, drawn off a stream).
