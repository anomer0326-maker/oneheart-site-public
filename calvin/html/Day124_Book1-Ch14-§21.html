Day 124 — Book 1, Chapter 14, §21

0 — Orientation (1 minute) –

Yesterday: look at the theatre. Today: how do you actually watch the show? §20 told us to take pious delight in the works of God. §21 gives the method. Calvin says "no need to linger on the end" — and then, in ten sentences, hands you a two-part rule for reading creation that the whole Reformed tradition will quote. This is the first half of a two-section unit: §21 = the first rule (never overlook God's perfections), §22 = the second rule (apply it to yourself, closer to faith).

Why examiners love it. Two inverted conditionals (S3, S10), a not only… but sentence with a triple-verb tail (S4), a semicolon rule with first… secondly… (S6), and an so… as to participle cascade (S7). The traps are scope (§21 illustrates only the first rule), both-conditions (S6 requires both, not either), and degree words (hasty… evanescent glance vs dwell long).

Today's 3 Big Points

  1. Inverted conditional Were one/we to V, … would/should V — S3 and S10. Formula 1.
  2. not only A, but B, C, and D — S4: one may not only run… but dwell…, turn…, and bring…. The emphasis falls on the tail. Formula 2.
  3. so V-ing … as to V / so… that result chains — S7: so admirably, that…, so stationing… that they cannot move, so tempering… as to measure out…. Formula 3.

Text traps. ⚠️ end (S1) = purpose, goal, not "finish". ⚠️ in due terms (S3) = in fitting terms. ⚠️ evanescent (S4) = quickly vanishing. ⚠️ didactic (S5) = meant for teaching. ⚠️ compendious (S6) = brief, summarizing (not "comprehensive"!). ⚠️ apprehension (S6) = grasp, understanding. ⚠️ self application (S6) = applying to oneself. ⚠️ revolutions (S8) = orbital turnings. ⚠️ miracles (S10) = wonders (L. mirari), not "violations of nature".

1 — Full Text (⚠️ invariable) — 10 sentences –

It is unnecessary to dwell at length on the end that should be aimed at in considering the works of God. The subject has been in a great measure explained elsewhere, and in so far as required by our present work, may now be disposed of in a few words. Undoubtedly were one to attempt to speak in due terms of the inestimable wisdom, power, justice, and goodness of God, in the formation of the world, no grace or splendour of diction could equal the greatness of the subject. Still there can be no doubt that the Lord would have us constantly occupied with such holy meditation, in order that, while we contemplate the immense treasures of wisdom and goodness exhibited in the creatures as in so many mirrors, we may not only run our eye over them with a hasty, and, as it were, evanescent glance, but dwell long upon them, seriously and faithfully turn them in our minds, and every now and then bring them to recollection. But as the present work is of a didactic nature, we cannot fittingly enter on topics which require lengthened discourse. Therefore, in order to be compendious, let the reader understand that he has a genuine apprehension of the character of God as the Creator of the world; first, if he attends to the general rule, never thoughtlessly or obliviously to overlook the glorious perfections which God displays in his creatures; and, secondly, if he makes a self application of what he sees, so as to fix it deeply on his heart. The former is exemplified when we consider how great the Architect must be who framed and ordered the multitude of the starry host so admirably, that it is impossible to imagine a more glorious sight, so stationing some, and fixing them to particular spots that they cannot move; giving a freer course to others yet setting limits to their wanderings; so tempering the movement of the whole as to measure out day and night, months, years, and seasons, and at the same time so regulating the inequality of days as to prevent every thing like confusion. The former course is, moreover, exemplified when we attend to his power in sustaining the vast mass, and guiding the swift revolutions of the heavenly bodies, &c. These few examples sufficiently explain what is meant by recognising the divine perfections in the creation of the world. Were we to attempt to go over the whole subject we should never come to a conclusion, there being as many miracles of divine power, as many striking evidences of wisdom and goodness, as there are classes of objects, nay, as there are individual objects, great or small, throughout the universe.

2 — Structure at a Glance – 

[OPENING]   S1–S2     No need to dwell on the end of studying God's works; it is largely explained elsewhere
[CONCESSION] S3       Eloquence can never equal the subject
[REVERSAL]  S4        STILL: the Lord wants steady meditation — not a hasty glance, but long dwelling
[LIMIT]     S5        This work is didactic: no lengthy discourse
[RULE]      S6        Two conditions for genuine apprehension of God as Creator:
                        (1) never overlook the perfections in creatures; (2) apply it to yourself
[EXAMPLES]  S7–S8     Rule 1 illustrated: the Architect's wisdom (S7), his power (S8)
[CLOSE]     S9–S10    A few examples suffice; the full survey would never end

Examiner's Eye. - ⚠️ Both-conditions trap (S6): a genuine apprehension needs first… and secondly…. An option saying either one suffices is wrong. - ⚠️ Scope trap (S7–S9): the starry examples illustrate the former rule only. Self-application (the second) is not illustrated in §21; it is taken up in §22. - ⚠️ Direction trap (S4): the hasty… glance is what we must not settle for. The Lord wants us to dwell long. - ⚠️ Concession trap (S3→S4): S3 says eloquence cannot equal the subject; S4 (Still…) says that does not excuse us from meditating. - ⚠️ Pairing trap (S7 vs S8): S7 = wisdom/order (the Architect arranging); S8 = power (sustaining, guiding). Swapping them is a classic wrong option.

One point. Dummy it + infinitive again (It is unnecessary to dwell…), and watch the relative clause that should be aimed at, which strands the preposition (aim at the end → the end that should be aimed at). ⚠️ end = goal, purpose.

S5★as the present work is of a didactic nature, we cannot fittingly enter on topics which require lengthened discourse›

🔤 Morphology · 어형
  • didactic — Gk. didaskein "teach" → Didache
전체 해설 더보기

One point. But as the present work is of a didactic nature — as = since (reason), not "while". Calvin's book is a teaching handbook, so we cannot fittingly enter on topics which require lengthened discourse = it would not be fitting to launch into topics needing long discussion. ⚠️ fittingly = appropriately (modest, not "unable").

4 — Today's Grammar Formulas –

Formula 1 — Inverted conditional (if deleted)

Were S to V, S would/should V…        = If S were to V, S would/should V…
Were one to attempt to speak…, no grace could equal…      (S3)
Were we to attempt to go over the whole subject we should never…   (S10)
⚠️ Delete `if`, move `were` before the subject. The main clause takes would/should/could.
⚠️ Don't put `if` and the inversion together: *If were we…* is wrong.

Drill: If we were to count every star, we should never finish. → Were we to count every star, we should never finish.

Formula 2 — not only A, but B, C, and D (tail-weighted)

S may not only V1 (…), but V2 (…), V3 (…), and V4 (…)        (S4)
⚠️ ONE `not only`; the `but` introduces ALL the following parallel verbs.
⚠️ The weight is on the `but` tail (what we SHOULD do), not on the `not only` item.

Drill: We should not only admire the heavens but ponder them, trace their order, and give thanks. (Calvin theme: creation and gratitude.)

Formula 3 — Result chains with so… as to / so… that

so ADV, that S V                       (S7: so admirably, that it is impossible…)
V-ing O to X, that S cannot V          (S7: fixing them to spots that they cannot move) — `that` = so that
so V-ing … as to V                     (S7: so tempering the movement… as to measure out day and night)
⚠️ `that` + its own subject (+ intransitive verb) = result clause, not a relative clause.
⚠️ `so… as to V` = in such a way as to V.

Drill: He so ordered the seasons as to sustain every living thing. / The stars are so ordered that no one can number them.

6 — Background in 5 Minutes –

The doctrine at stake. §21 is the first half of a two-step answer to the question: what is the point of studying creation? Calvin says there are two ways to consider God's works. The first (§21) is to see God's perfections — wisdom, power, justice, goodness — in the order of the world, and never to overlook them. The second (§22) comes "closer to faith": seeing that God made all things for our good and salvation, and feeling his power and grace in ourselves, which stirs trust, invocation, praise and love. S6 states both; §21 illustrates only the first.

Lineage. The idea that creation is a mirror of God's attributes appears earlier in the Institutes (I.5.1, I.5.9) and has roots in Psalm 19, Romans 1:20, and the Augustinian habit of reading creation as a "book". Calvin's own list wisdom, power, justice, goodness is his standard summary of God's attributes displayed in the world (compare I.5.6–10). The astronomical examples (fixed stars, wandering planets, seasons) reflect the astronomy of his day, which Calvin treats as a display of order, not as a scientific lecture; his later Genesis commentary and the Hexaemeron tradition of Basil and Ambrose (§20) are where the details live.

Later reception. Reformed confessions echo the "book of creation" idea: the Belgic Confession (1561), art. 2, describes creation as a most beautiful book before our eyes. The two-step rule — observe, then apply to yourself — also shapes the Reformed habit of linking natural wonder to personal gratitude. Treat later systems as later: S1–S10 itself gives only the two-condition rule and a few examples.

Over-reading guardrail. ⚠️ §21 does not claim that studying the stars alone yields a "genuine apprehension" of God: S6 requires both conditions, and the second is application to the heart. ⚠️ Nor does it say that eloquence is useless (S3 says only that it cannot equal the subject). ⚠️ And not only… but… (S4) does not forbid an initial glance; it says a glance is not enough.

7 — Scripture Connections –

  1. S1–S4 — Ps 19:1–6; Rom 1:20. "The heavens declare the glory of God"; "the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made" (τὰ ἀόρατα… τοῖς ποιήμασιν νοούμενα καθορᾶται). Calvin's mirrors language is built on these two texts. He uses them as the warrant for looking at creation, not as a proof that creation alone saves.
  2. S4 — Ps 77:12; Ps 143:5. "I will meditate also of all thy work"; "I remember the days of old; I meditate on all thy works." dwell long upon them… bring them to recollection is the Psalmists' meditating and remembering.
  3. S4 — Ps 111:2; Ps 92:4–5. "The works of the LORD are great, sought out of all them that have pleasure therein"; "O LORD, how great are thy works!" The holy meditation is delighted, careful seeking out.
  4. S7 — Ps 8:3; Isa 40:26. "When I consider thy heavens, the work of thy fingers, the moon and the stars, which thou hast ordained"; "Lift up your eyes on high… that bringeth out their host by number: he calleth them all by names." The starry host, ordered and named, is Isaiah's image, and his by the greatness of his might is exactly the wisdom-plus-power pairing of S7–S8.
  5. S7 — Gen 1:14–18; Ps 104:19; Job 38:31–33. Lights for signs, and for seasons, and for days, and years; "He appointed the moon for seasons"; "Canst thou bind the sweet influences of Pleiades… knowest thou the ordinances of heaven?" The day and night, months, years, and seasons of S7.
  6. S8 — Heb 1:3; Col 1:17; Ps 147:4–5. "Upholding all things by the word of his power"; "by him all things consist"; "He telleth the number of the stars." The power in sustaining the vast mass is continuing providence, not only an initial act.

Calvin's habit here: he points to the Psalms and Isaiah rather than quoting them at length, and uses Scripture's own list of heavenly wonders as the pattern for the examples.

8 — Exam Problems (출제자의 눈) –

① Grammar — which statement about the grammar of §21 is wrong?

(A) In S3, were one to attempt… is an inverted conditional equivalent to if one were to attempt…. (B) In S4, we may not only run our eye… but dwell long upon them, … turn them in our minds, and… bring them to recollection has one not only followed by three parallel verbs after but. (C) In S7, fixing them to particular spots that they cannot move, the clause that they cannot move is a relative clause modifying spots. (D) In S10, there being as many miracles… as there are classes of objects is an absolute construction with its own subject there.

Answer: (C). Rationale: that they cannot move is a result clause (that = so that: the stars are fixed so that they cannot move), with its own subject they and an intransitive move; it is not a relative clause on spots. (A) Formula 1. (B) Formula 2. (D) S10 absolute.

② Comprehension — which statement is consistent with §21?

(A) Calvin says that, because the subject is so great, he will treat the works of God at length in this book. (B) Calvin says a reader has a genuine apprehension of God as Creator if he never overlooks the perfections God displays in his creatures and also applies what he sees to himself. (C) Calvin says the examples of the stars in S7–S8 illustrate the second rule, self-application. (D) Calvin says a hasty glance at the creatures is enough, provided the reader takes it to heart.

Answer: (B). Rationale: (A) scope/direction flip — S5: the work is didactic, so Calvin cannot fittingly give lengthened discourse. (C) scope-overreach — the stars illustrate the former (S7, S8); the second rule is not illustrated here. (D) direction flip — S4: a hasty… evanescent glance is exactly what is not enough; the Lord wants us to dwell long.

③ Composition — apply Formula 1 (inverted conditional):

Rewrite with inversion: If we were to try to list every wonder of creation, we should never finish.

Model answer: Were we to try to list every wonder of creation, we should never finish. Rationale: delete if, put were before we; keep should in the main clause.

9 — One-Line Wrap-up + Homework –

One-line summary: No eloquence can equal the subject (inverted conditional), yet the Lord wants us to dwell on his works, not just glance at them (not only… but…); a genuine apprehension of God as Creator needs both never overlooking his perfections and applying them to ourselves, and the stars show the first — his wisdom and power — though an exhaustive tour would never end (inverted conditional + so… as to).
