Day 38 — Book 1, Chapter 8, §2

0 — Orientation (1 minute) –

Yesterday Calvin argued that the Bible's bad prose is a design feature: the sublime mysteries of the kingdom of heaven have for the greater part been delivered with a contemptible meanness of words, and an unpolished simplicity, almost bordering on rudeness, makes a deeper impression than the loftiest flights of oratory.

Today, sentence one:

I confess, however, that in elegance and beauty, nay, splendour, the style of some of the prophets is not surpassed by the eloquence of heathen writers.

Read that cold and it looks like a man walking his own argument back twenty-four hours after making it. It is not. Go back to yesterday's S6 and find four words you probably read straight past: for the greater part. ⚠️ He built the exit before he needed it. That is what a limiting phrase is for, and if you did not mark it yesterday, mark it now — this is how Calvin writes: the qualification is planted one section early, quietly, and then cashed.

But the concession is not merely defensive housekeeping. Watch what he does with it in S2. The elegant prophets are not an embarrassment to be managed; they are turned into evidence:

   IF the Bible were plain everywhere        → "he could not write better"
   BUT some prophets write like Isaiah       → "he could, and elsewhere chose not to"
        ↓
   S2:  it was NOT FROM WANT OF ELOQUENCE  ‹that›  he in other instances
        used a rude and homely style

The splendour of Isaiah is what proves the roughness of Amos was a choice, not a ceiling. Calvin grants the opponent's strongest ground and then stands on it. That is his method in the whole chapter, and today is the cleanest specimen of it in Book 1.

And then S3 delivers the thesis that makes the entire argument-from-majesty coherent: whether you read David's sweetness or Amos the herdsman's rougher idiom, **that majesty of the Spirit to which I adverted appears conspicuous in all**. ★ Which means majesty is not a stylistic property at all. If it appears equally in two opposite styles, it is not made of style. It is what shows through style — and that is exactly why it can serve as evidence, and exactly why an "aesthetic apologetic" reading of §1 was wrong.

Then the section walks straight into the hardest objection in the whole of Christian apologetics, and Calvin does not flinch from it — he raises it himself, at length, in the longest sentence of the day: ⚠️ Satan apes God. Counterfeit revelation exists; it also uses an uncouth and semi-barbarous style; it also employs obsolete forms of expression. If rough archaic language is a mark of divinity, the oracles have it too. Watch, in S5, the single word he uses to dispose of the counterfeit — **affectation** — and ask yourself, honestly, whether one sentence is enough.

Today's 3 Big Points — write them down now:

  1. The cleft sentence with the that amputated. S2: it was **not from want of eloquence** ‹**that**› he in other instances used a rude and homely style. Two traps in one clause. ⚠️ First, want = LACK, not desire — the archaic reversal the syllabus keeps warning you about, and today is where it decides the meaning of the section. ⚠️ Second, the connector that is deleted, so he in other instances used looks like a fresh main clause and the sentence appears to have two of them. It has one.
  2. as ... so ... correlative — with a purpose clause folded inside it, and a comparative adverb wearing a definite article. S4: **as** Satan often apes God, (**that he may** by a fallacious resemblance **the better** insinuate himself into the minds of the simple,) **so** he craftily disseminated the impious errors.... Three separate constructions stacked in one 60-word sentence. ⚠️ that he may = so that he may (purpose, not that-clause). ⚠️ the better insinuate = the more effectively insinuate — the + comparative adverb, the same the as in the more, the merrier.
  3. Negation through the back door — six times in eight sentences. is **not surpassed** by (S1) · **not** from **want** of eloquence (S2) · I am **not unaware** (S4) · **None** ... **need** be told (S5) · man **never could have** conceived (S6) · **not one** will be found who does **not** rise (S7). ⚠️ Four of these are litotes — a negative that means the positive with extra force — and S7 is a double negative that means a universal affirmative. If you translate these flat, you will end up with a Calvin who is hedging. He is doing the opposite.

And one small beauty to watch for: the section opens on **savours** of rusticity (S3) and closes on **insipid** ... devoid of **taste** (S8). ★ sapere at both ends. Calvin sealed the section with a taste-bracket. Eight sentences. Let's go.

1 — Full Text (the whole § — 8 sentences, verbatim) –

2. I confess, however, that in elegance and beauty, nay, splendour, the style of some of the prophets is not surpassed by the eloquence of heathen writers. By examples of this description, the Holy Spirit was pleased to show that it was not from want of eloquence he in other instances used a rude and homely style. But whether you read David, Isaiah, and others of the same class, whose discourse flows sweet and pleasant; or Amos the herdsman, Jeremiah, and Zechariah, whose rougher idiom savours of rusticity; that majesty of the Spirit to which I adverted appears conspicuous in all. I am not unaware, that as Satan often apes God, that he may by a fallacious resemblance the better insinuate himself into the minds of the simple, so he craftily disseminated the impious errors with which he deceived miserable men in an uncouth and semi-barbarous style, and frequently employed obsolete forms of expression in order to cloak his impostures. None possessed of any moderate share of sense need be told how vain and vile such affectation is. But in regard to the Holy Scriptures, however petulant men may attempt to carp at them, they are replete with sentiments which it is clear that man never could have conceived. Let each of the prophets be examined, and not one will be found who does not rise far higher than human reach. Examiner's Eye — four traps, and the first one is worth the whole section.

⚠️ ① "S1 retracts §1." The single most likely 내용일치 distractor, and it is designed to catch the student who read yesterday without marking for the greater part. S1 does not withdraw yesterday's claim; S2 conscripts S1 into it. The elegance of some prophets is the premise of an argument for the providential character of the roughness of the others. Concession → weapon, in one sentence.

⚠️ ② "Calvin admits the counterfeits are indistinguishable." S4 is long, vivid, and entirely about Satan; a hurried reader finishes it with the impression that Calvin has conceded the parity. S5 exists precisely to deny it — and note that S5 denies it by refusing to argue: None possessed of any moderate share of sense **need be told**. Whether that refusal satisfies you is Period 6's business. But do not misreport what the text says.

⚠️ ③ S7's double negative. not one will be found who does not rise far higher than human reach = every single one rises far higher. A distractor reading "some prophets do not rise above human reach" flips it, and the flip is invisible if you read the sentence at speed.

⚠️ ④ S1's some. the style of **some** of the prophets. Any option saying all the prophets are elegant — or all are rough — is dead on the quantifier alone. Calvin's whole point in S3 is that they come in both kinds.

6 — Background in 5 Minutes –

① Why §2 exists at all — and why it is not a retraction.

Yesterday's argument had a hole in it the size of Isaiah. If Scripture's authority is evidenced by a contemptible meanness of words, what do you do with the prophets who write magnificently? Calvin had already covered it with a four-word limiter — for the greater part — and §2 is where the limiter is cashed. But look at the structure of the cashing, because it is the same three-beat move as yesterday's you will, I admit, feel wonderfully allured:

   BEAT 1  GRANT the opponent's strongest ground, and inflate it        (S1: "nay, splendour")
   BEAT 2  SHOW the granted ground supports your own case               (S2: "not from want of eloquence")
   BEAT 3  RELOCATE the claim where the concession cannot reach it      (S3: majesty is in ALL)

★ This is Calvin's standard operating procedure in Chapter 8, and once you see it you will see it four more times before the chapter ends. A concession that you make larger than your opponent asked for is a concession he can no longer use.

② Majesty is not a stylistic property — and this is the load-bearing claim of the whole chapter.

S3 is easy to skim and impossible to overrate. If that majesty of the Spirit ... appears conspicuous **in all** — in David's sweetness and in Amos's rusticity — then majesty is not elegance, and not plainness either. It is the constant across an inverted variable.

⚠️ Note a mild tension with the Westminster Confession here, and do not paper over it. WCF 1.5 lists among the evidences the **majesty of the style**. Read on its own that phrase locates majesty in style, which is precisely what Calvin's S3 denies. The Divines are compressing; Calvin is discriminating. When a confession summarises a theologian, the summary is usually shorter than the thought — which is a reason to read the theologian.

③ Amos the herdsman — a real judgment about Hebrew, and a guardrail on it.

Amos 1:1 (among the herdmen of Tekoa, נֹקְדִים) and 7:14 (I was no prophet ... but I was an herdman, and a gatherer of sycomore fruit) give Calvin his epithet, and the choice is pointed: the one prophet who disclaims professional standing in his own text is made the test case for style. Jeremiah and Zechariah round out the "rough" column.

⚠️ Guardrail. Calvin's stylistic rankings reflect the Hebrew scholarship of his century, and later Hebraists have argued the other way — Amos's Hebrew is now widely regarded as rhetorically sophisticated (the oracle-cycle structure of chs. 1–2, the doxologies, the vision sequence). Do not turn Calvin's literary impressions into linguistic doctrine. Note also that his argument does not actually depend on the rankings being right: he needs only that the prophetic corpus contains conspicuously different registers, which is uncontroversial. The premise survives even if the examples are re-sorted.

④ Simia Dei — Satan the ape of God.

as Satan often apes God is not Calvin's coinage; it is a long-standing commonplace. Tertullian, De Praescriptione Haereticorum 40, argues that the devil imitates the sacraments of the divine mysteries in the pagan rites — baptism, marking of the forehead, an offering of bread, a resurrection — precisely in order to compete. Augustine works the same theme against pagan theurgy. By the Reformation simia Dei is standard vocabulary on all sides.

The biblical charter is 2 Cor. 11:13–15: false apostles transforming themselves (μετασχηματιζόμενοι) into apostles of Christ, for Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light. ★ Note what Paul's verb concedes: the transformation works. The counterfeit really does look like the thing. Calvin is not being alarmist; he is quoting the New Testament's own estimate of the difficulty.

⑤ Stylistic forensics was a live Renaissance method — and that is what S5 is invoking.

When Calvin says Satan frequently employed **obsolete forms of expression** in order to **cloak** his impostures, and then dismisses it as **affectation**, he is standing on a technique his century had just perfected: you can detect a forgery by its style. Lorenzo Valla's Declamatio (1440) demolished the Donation of Constantine by showing its Latin was pretending to be older than it was — anachronistic vocabulary, wrong idiom, a fourth-century document written in language nobody spoke in the fourth century. Erasmus applied comparable philological pressure to patristic attributions.

★ So S5's None possessed of any moderate share of sense need be told how vain and vile such affectation is is not a shrug. It is the humanist claim that studied archaism reads as studied to a trained eye. Whether it is sufficient against S4 is another question — see ⑥.

⑥ ⚠️ 과대해석 방지 — five lines you must not cross with today's text.

  1. §2 is not a retraction of §1. S1's some and yesterday's for the greater part are the same limiter, and S2 makes the concession serve the original argument. Anyone quoting S1 as "Calvin admits the plain-style argument fails" has stopped reading after one sentence.
  2. Majesty ≠ beauty. S3 makes majesty the constant across both registers. Any use of this section to argue "the Bible is beautiful, therefore divine" is defeated by the sentence it would be quoting.
  3. Calvin does not here refute the counterfeit objection; he dismisses it. Be honest about this. S4 concedes the phenomenon in sixty words; S5 disposes of it in fifteen, and disposes of it by declining to argue (None ... need be told). The substantive answer arrives only in S6, when the criterion moves from style to content — sentiments which ... man never could have conceived. ⚠️ If you are going to use this section apologetically, S6 is the load-bearing sentence, not S5.
  4. S8 is not a claim about intelligence. devoid of taste sits inside Calvin's running sensory epistemology (I.5.3 tasted his paternal love; I.7.2 sweet and bitter ... of taste), and its doctrinal ground is 1 Cor. 2:14 — the natural man receiveth not the things of the Spirit of God ... neither can he know them, because they are **spiritually discerned**. The incapacity is located in the absence of the Spirit's testimony (Ch. 7), not in the reader's IQ. Read as an insult it is merely rude; read in context it is I.7.4–5 restated in the vocabulary of the palate.
  5. Do not read S7's not one will be found as a survey result. It is a challenge in the form of a prediction (see S7 §2). Calvin is inviting the examination, not reporting one.

⑦ Where the modern conversation picks this up.

S4 is, in contemporary dress, the parity objection: if a belief may be held as properly basic on the ground of an inner witness, what stops any rival tradition from claiming the same warrant? Plantinga's readers know it as the "Great Pumpkin objection," and the standard reply — that a properly basic belief remains open to defeaters, and that a parity claim must actually be made good case by case rather than merely asserted — is a development of Calvin, not a quotation of him. ★ Calvin raises the objection and answers it with S6's criterion (content beyond human reach) plus S5's stylistic forensics. Whether that is enough is a real question, and a student who can state it without either sneering at Calvin or pretending he settled it has understood the section.

② 내용일치 — §2의 내용과 일치하는 것은?

  1. Calvin withdraws the argument of the preceding section: since the style of some prophets rivals that of the pagan writers, the plainness of the sacred writings can no longer be treated as any mark of their divine origin.
  2.   3. Calvin concedes that, since Satan clothes his errors in the same uncouth and antiquated style, the counterfeits cannot be told apart from Scripture by any faculty available to ordinary human sense.

9 — One-Line Wrap-up + Homework –

One-line summary. Calvin opens by making the enemy's case for him — **I confess**, however, that in elegance and beauty, **nay**, splendour, the style of **some** of the prophets is **not surpassed by** the eloquence of heathen writers — and then spends the next sentence turning that concession into ammunition: By examples **of this description**, the Holy Spirit **was pleased to show** that it was **not from want of eloquence** ‹that› he in other instances used a **rude and homely** style (⚠️ want = lack, and the cleft's that has been cut out), so that Isaiah's splendour becomes the proof that Amos's roughness was a choice, not a ceiling; then comes the thesis, delayed behind two semicolons — **But whether** you read David, Isaiah ... **whose discourse flows sweet and pleasant**; **or** Amos **the herdsman**, Jeremiah, and Zechariah, **whose rougher idiom savours of rusticity**; **that majesty of the Spirit to which I adverted appears conspicuous in all** — which, since the two registers are opposites, establishes that majesty is not a stylistic property at all; whereupon Calvin raises against himself the one objection every argument from marks must face, and raises it in his longest sentence — I am **not unaware**, that **as** Satan often **apes** God, (**that he may** by a **fallacious resemblance the better insinuate** himself into the minds of **the simple**,) **so** he craftily **disseminated** the impious errors ... in an **uncouth and semi-barbarous** style, and frequently employed **obsolete forms of expression in order to cloak his impostures** — concedes the phenomenon entirely, and then disposes of it with one noun and no argument: **None** possessed of any moderate share of sense **need be told** how **vain and vile** such **affectation** is (Satan's plainness is put on; Scripture's is not); and only then does he give the substantive answer, by moving the criterion off style and onto content — **But** in regard to the Holy Scriptures, **however petulant** men **may attempt to carp** at them, they are **replete with sentiments which** ‹**it is clear that**› **man never could have conceived** — since a style can be copied but a thought beyond human reach cannot; and he closes, as he closed yesterday, with an experiment and a verdict: **Let** each of the prophets **be examined**, **and not one** will be found **who does not rise** far higher than **human reach**. Those who feel their works **insipid** must be absolutely **devoid of taste** — the last word, insipid, carrying the same root as savours in S3, so that the section is sealed at both ends by the vocabulary of the palate.

And the three grammar points, once more:

  1. The cleft with its that amputated — plus the archaic reversal that decides the doctrine. It was **not from want of eloquence** ‹**that**› he in other instances used a rude and homely style (S2). ⚠️ When you meet it is/was + adverbial + a full clause with no connector, restore the that first. ⚠️ And want = lack (< ON vanta, kin to wane, vanus) — cf. for want of a nail, found wanting. The whole argument turns on could have, chose not to, and that reading exists only if want means lack.
  2. as ... so ..., with a purpose clause folded in and a comparative wearing a definite article. **as** Satan often apes God, (**that he may** ... **the better** insinuate himself ...) **so** he craftily disseminated ... (S4). ⚠️ Sixty words between as and so; lose the correlative and the analogy collapses into narrative. ⚠️ that he may = so that (purpose) — the same sentence's **in order to** cloak is the modern form of the identical relation. ⚠️ the better = by that much more effectively; the the is the Old English instrumental, as in the more, the merrier. Reading technique: delete the comma-fenced material, find the spine, put it back.
  3. Four ways to say a thing by negating its opposite. ① Litotes — is **not surpassed** by (≥, S1) · **not** from **want** of (S2) · I am **not unaware** (S4); ⚠️ litotes in argumentative prose is usually a concession marker — go find the but. ② Modal need — **None** ... **need be** told (S5): bare infinitive, no -s, and it is the negative subject that licenses it (*needs be told ✗). ③ Relative + inserted judgment — sentiments **which** ‹**it is clear that**› man never could have conceived (S6): the that belongs to it is clear; cover the insertion with your finger and the relative clause is flawless. ④ Double negative = universal affirmative — **not one** will be found **who does not rise** far higher than human reach (S7) = every single one rises; bracket both negations on paper, never in your head — and note the frame is yesterday's conditional imperative, Let ... be examined, **and** ....

Weigh the chapters one by one, and not one will be found which does not ask more of the reader than he brought." — then check your work against S2 for the deleted that and the meaning of want, against S4 for as ... that he may ... the better ... so ... in order to, and against S5/S7 for modal need and the double negative.

The literary argument is over; Calvin says so in his first breath — **As this subject has been treated at large by others, it will be sufficient here merely to touch on its leading points** — and then turns to the second of the chapter's four parts: the argument from antiquity. ⚠️ Watch for the pivot phrase **In addition to the qualities already mentioned, great weight is due to the antiquity of Scripture**, and then a genuinely clever historical move: Moses **does not introduce a new Deity** but leads Israel **back to the covenant which had been made with Abraham** — so the claim is not merely that Moses is old, but that Moses appeals to something already older than himself, and appeals to it publicly, before people who would have known if he were inventing it (their deliverance from the bondage in which they were held must have been a fact of **familiar and universal notoriety**). ★ That is an argument from the impossibility of a successful forgery in front of witnesses — the same forensic instinct as today's affectation, moved from style to history.
