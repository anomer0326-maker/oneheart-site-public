Day 57 — Book 1, Chapter 11, §2

0 — Orientation (1 minute) –

Yesterday Calvin laid the ruling: no shape, no comparison, no exceptions. He quoted Exodus 20:4 and slammed the door. But a ruling without reasons is just an assertion — and Calvin knows it. So §2 opens with one word that tells you exactly what today is: "inferred."

Today is the evidence hearing.

And watch how he builds the witness list. It is not random. It is a deliberate escalation in four steps:

  1. Moses — the Law itself (Deut. 4:15).
  2. Isaiah — the prophets (five citations, fired off like a burst).
  3. Paul — the apostles (Acts 17:29).
  4. Seneca — a pagan. A hostile witness. Calvin has done this before (Cicero in I.3.1, Diagoras in I.3.3), and he does it again here, and it is always his favourite move: the defence's own witness convicts the defendant.

Then, having stacked heaven and earth against images, he turns and executes the one escape route the image-defenders have left: "but that prohibition was only for the Jews — they were especially prone to superstition." Calvin kills it twice. Once with a principle (S10). Once with a fact (S11). And S11 is eleven words of pure knockout. Learn it.

Grammatically, this section is a feast. S4 is one of the finest ellipsis structures in the whole book. S9 is Latinate word-order in English. S10 is the counterfactual as if in its purest form. Don't skim any of them.

Today's 3 Big Points

  1. Gapping — verb ellipsis across a parallel series (S4, S6). he who is incorporeal **is assimilated** to corporeal matter; he who is invisible **[is assimilated]** to a visible image; … Three of the four members have no verb at all. If you hunt for one you will stall. Learn to carry the verb forward.
  2. as if + past subjunctive = counterfactual contempt (S10). as if a prohibition … **could** be restricted to a single nation. This as if does not introduce a possibility. It introduces something Calvin regards as flatly false, and the past-tense modal is the tell.
  3. Object fronting (OSV) for emphasis (S9) — The sacred immortal, and invisible gods **they exhibit** in the meanest … materials — plus its rhetorical cousin, the question that asserts (S7): And is it strange that …? = "It is not strange at all."

Bonus trap I will absolutely test you on: responses in S7 does not mean "replies." And pretend in S10 does not mean "make believe." Two archaic reversals in one section.

1 — Full Text (⚠️ invariable) — 11 sentences –

2. This may easily be inferred from the reasons which he annexes to his prohibition. First, it is said in the books of Moses (Deut. 4:15), “Take ye therefore good heed unto yourselves; for ye saw no manner of similitude in the day that the Lord spake unto you in Horeb, out of the midst of the fire, lest ye corrupt yourselves, and make you a graven image, the similitude of any figure,” &c. We see how plainly God declares against all figures, to make us aware that all longing after such visible shapes is rebellion against him. Of the prophets, it will be sufficient to mention Isaiah, who is the most copious on this subjects (Isaiah 40:18; 41:7, 29; 45:9; 46:5), in order to show how the majesty of God is defiled by an absurd and indecorous fiction, when he who is incorporeal is assimilated to corporeal matter; he who is invisible to a visible image; he who is a spirit to an inanimate object; and he who fills all space to a bit of paltry wood, or stone, or gold. Paul, too, reasons in the same way, “Forasmuch, then, as we are the offspring of God, we ought not to think that the Godhead is like unto gold, or silver, or stone, graven by art and man’s device,” (Acts 17:29). Hence it is manifest, that whatever statues are set up or pictures painted to represent God, are utterly displeasing to him, as a kind of insults to his majesty. And is it strange that the Holy Spirit thunders such responses from heaven, when he compels even blind and miserable idolaters to make a similar confession on the earth? Seneca’s complaint, as given by Augustine De Civit. Dei, c. 10, is well known. He says “The sacred immortal, and invisible gods they exhibit in the meanest and most ignoble materials, and dress them in the clothing of men and beasts; some confound the sexes, and form a compound out of different bodies, giving the name of deities to objects, which, if they were met alive, would be deemed monsters.” Hence, again, it is obvious, that the defenders of images resort to a paltry quibbling evasion, when they pretend that the Jews were forbidden to use them on account of their proneness to superstition; as if a prohibition which the Lord founds on his own eternal essences and the uniform course of nature, could be restricted to a single nation. Besides, when Paul refuted the error of giving a bodily shape to God, he was addressing not Jews, but Athenians.

Sentence count: 11 (S1–S11). One paragraph.

Source housekeeping (page furniture removed, text otherwise character-exact): page marker 92 sat at the end of the line ending …pictures painted to represent God,; running headers for pages 96–97 and footnote 26 (the French addition about onions and leeks, belonging to §1) intervene in the scan and are removed. No hyphen repairs were needed in §2. Two apparent oddities are the source's own and are preserved verbatim: who is the most copious on this subjects (for this subject) and his own eternal essences (plural; the Latin is singular essentia). Curly quotation marks and &c. are the source's own.

2 — Structure at a Glance (board work) – 

[FRAME]     S1   The prohibition carries its own REASONS → they can be inferred
[WITNESS 1] S2   MOSES  — Deut 4:15: "ye saw no manner of similitude"
            S3   ⤷ inference: longing after visible shapes = REBELLION
[WITNESS 2] S4   ISAIAH — 5 refs; the 4-rung ladder of category-violation
                   incorporeal → corporeal │ invisible → visible
                   spirit     → inanimate  │ omnipresent → a bit of wood
[WITNESS 3] S5   PAUL   — Acts 17:29: offspring of God ≠ gold/silver/stone
[VERDICT]   S6   ∴ ALL statues & pictures of God = insults to his majesty
[HOSTILE    S7   Q: strange that heaven thunders, when EARTH confesses too?
 WITNESS]   S8   SENECA (via Augustine, De Civ. Dei) — "well known"
            S9   ⤷ his own words: ignoble matter, human dress, monsters
[EVASION    S10  Escape route: "it was only for the superstition-prone JEWS"
 KILLED]         ⤷ refuted by GROUND: eternal essence + course of nature
            S11  ⤷ refuted by FACT: Paul said it to ATHENIANS

Examiner's Eye — four traps loaded:

  1. S1 "may easily be inferred" — scope trap. An option will read "Calvin admits God gave no reason for the prohibition." The opposite. annexes means God attached reasons to it. The reasons are in the text; Calvin is reading them out, not supplying them.
  2. S4 ellipsis trap — the most likely grammar item. A test will ask you to identify the verb governing he who is invisible **to** a visible image. Answer: is assimilated, carried forward from the first member. A wrong option will try to make is invisible the governing verb — which would give you the nonsense "he is invisible to a visible image."
  3. S7 word trap — responses. Here it means oracles / divine pronouncements (Latin responsa — the technical word for an oracle's answer). An option that glosses it as "the Holy Spirit's replies to prayer" is wrong. And thunders is not decoration: it sets up heaven against S9's earth.
  4. S10/S11 direction-flip. An option will read "Calvin concedes the second commandment was addressed to Israel's particular weakness." That is exactly the position he calls a paltry quibbling evasion. S11 is the counter-fact: Paul preached it to Greeks.

3 — Sentence-by-Sentence Live Teaching –

S1★★the frame word: annexes — and what "inferred" commits Calvin to›

This may easily be inferred from the reasons which he annexes to his prohibition.

🔤 Morphology · 어형
  • infer < in + ferre (to carry in) — to carry a conclusion in from evidence; contrast imply < in + plicare (to fold in), which is what the text does. annex < ad + nectere (to bind on) — cognate with connect, nexus. prohibition < pro + habere (to hold forward, hold back) — the same habere under inhibit, exhibit (which returns in S9!).
💬 Idiom · 관용
  • may easily be inferred = can readily be deduced; annex A to B = attach A onto B as an integral part.
쉬운 영어 / Modern English

You can easily work this out from the reasons God attaches to the prohibition itself.

Key changes · 올·현대 표현
  • may easily be inferred → can easily be worked out
  • annexes to → attaches to
  • his prohibition → the ban he gives.
S2★★★KJV archaism density — and the lest clause that carries the argument›

First, it is said in the books of Moses (Deut. 4:15), “Take ye therefore good heed unto yourselves; for ye saw no manner of similitude in the day that the Lord spake unto you in Horeb, out of the midst of the fire, lest ye corrupt yourselves, and make you a graven image, the similitude of any figure,” &c.

🔤 Morphology · 어형
  • similitude < similis (like) + -tudo — likeness, form; cognate simulate, assimilate (which arrives in S4!). graven = past participle of grave (to carve, engrave) — Old English grafan, cognate with German graben (to dig); nothing to do with grave = tomb, though both go back to "dig." heed — Old English hēdan (to care for); noun here. corrupt < cor- (com-) + rumpere (to break) — break to pieces, not merely "make dishonest."
💬 Idiom · 관용
  • take heed unto/to = pay close attention to; no manner of X = no X whatsoever; lest = so that … not, for fear that; make you a X = make yourselves a X (ethical dative); &c. = et cetera.
쉬운 영어 / Modern English

First, Moses records (Deut. 4:15): "So watch yourselves very carefully. You saw no form at all on the day the LORD spoke to you at Horeb out of the fire — so don't ruin yourselves by carving an image for yourselves in any shape," and so on.

Key changes · 올·현대 표현
  • take ye good heed unto yourselves → watch yourselves carefully
  • no manner of similitude → no form at all
  • spake → spoke
  • lest ye corrupt yourselves → so that you don't ruin yourselves
  • make you a graven image → carve yourselves an image
  • &c. → and so on.
S3★★the inference — and the word rebellion, which is stronger than you think›

We see how plainly God declares against all figures, to make us aware that all longing after such visible shapes is rebellion against him.

🔤 Morphology · 어형
  • declare < de + clarare (to make clear) — from clarus (bright, clear); declare against keeps the forensic sense. rebellion < re + bellum (war again) — literally renewed war against a ruler; Calvin means it. aware — Old English gewær (watchful), same root as wary, beware.
💬 Idiom · 관용
  • declare against = rule/pronounce against; how plainly = just how clearly; longing after = craving for (archaic after where modern English says for); make someone aware that = make someone realise that.
쉬운 영어 / Modern English

We can see how clearly God rules out every image, to make us realise that any craving for visible forms of him is an act of rebellion against him.

Key changes · 올·현대 표현
  • declares against → rules out
  • how plainly → how clearly
  • longing after → craving for
  • rebellion against him → an act of rebellion against him.
S4★★★the ellipsis ladder — today's headline structure›

Of the prophets, it will be sufficient to mention Isaiah, who is the most copious on this subjects (Isaiah 40:18; 41:7, 29; 45:9; 46:5), in order to show how the majesty of God is defiled by an absurd and indecorous fiction, when he who is incorporeal is assimilated to corporeal matter; he who is invisible to a visible image; he who is a spirit to an inanimate object; and he who fills all space to a bit of paltry wood, or stone, or gold.

🔤 Morphology · 어형
  • copious < copia (abundance) — cf. cornucopia, copy (originally "abundance of transcripts"). indecorous < in- + decorus (fitting, seemly) — from decor, cognate with decent, decorate, dignity. assimilate < ad + similis (toward likeness) — the very root of §2's similitude in S2; Calvin is echoing Moses' own word. incorporeal < in- + corpus (body). inanimate < in- + anima (breath, soul). paltry — probably from a dialectal palt (rag, trash); "trashy." fiction < fingere (to mould clay) — cf. figment, feign, effigy.
💬 Idiom · 관용
  • of the prophets = among/out of the prophets; it will be sufficient to V = it is enough just to V; be copious on a subject = treat it at great length; assimilate A to B = make A like B, liken A to B; a bit of = a scrap of.
쉬운 영어 / Modern English

Among the prophets, it's enough to cite Isaiah, who says the most about this (Isa. 40:18; 41:7, 29; 45:9; 46:5), to show how God's majesty is degraded by a ridiculous and unfitting invention — when the bodiless God is likened to physical matter, the invisible God to a visible picture, the God who is spirit to a lifeless object, and the God who fills all space to a scrap of cheap wood or stone or gold.

Key changes · 올·현대 표현
  • of the prophets → among the prophets
  • copious on this subject → says the most about this
  • defiled → degraded
  • indecorous fiction → unfitting invention
  • assimilated to → likened to
  • paltry → cheap, worthless.
S5★★Forasmuch … as — and why Paul's Greek word matters for S11›

Paul, too, reasons in the same way, “Forasmuch, then, as we are the offspring of God, we ought not to think that the Godhead is like unto gold, or silver, or stone, graven by art and man’s device,” (Acts 17:29).

🔤 Morphology · 어형
  • forasmuch as — a frozen compound of for + as + much + as; survives only in legal/liturgical English. offspring — Old English ofspring, "that which springs off"; renders Greek γένος (kin, stock, race). device < Old French deviser (to divide, arrange, plan) — hence "scheme, contrivance"; not "apparatus." graven — again the carving verb from S2; the KJV keeps the Second-Commandment word deliberately.
💬 Idiom · 관용
  • forasmuch as = since, seeing that; reason in the same way = argue along the same lines; be like unto X = resemble X (archaic unto for to); man's device = human contrivance/imagination.
쉬운 영어 / Modern English

Paul argues the same way: "Since we are God's offspring, we shouldn't suppose that the divine being is like gold or silver or stone, shaped by human skill and imagination" (Acts 17:29).

Key changes · 올·현대 표현
  • Forasmuch, then, as → Since
  • the Godhead → the divine being
  • like unto → like
  • graven by art and man's device → shaped by human skill and imagination.
S6★★whatever + a second gapping — and the word insults›

Hence it is manifest, that whatever statues are set up or pictures painted to represent God, are utterly displeasing to him, as a kind of insults to his majesty.

🔤 Morphology · 어형
  • manifest < manus (hand) + -festus (struck) — "hit by the hand," i.e. palpable, obvious. insult < in + salire (to leap upon) — an assault, not merely rude speech; cf. assault, salient, resilient. majesty < maiestas < maior (greater) — in Roman law crimen laesae maiestatis = treason. Calvin's word choice is legal, and it makes S3's rebellion the same charge.
💬 Idiom · 관용
  • hence it is manifest that = so it is obvious that; set up a statue = erect a statue; a kind of X = so many X's, something in the nature of X.
쉬운 영어 / Modern English

So it's clear that any statue erected or picture painted to represent God is completely offensive to him — they're insults to his majesty.

Key changes · 올·현대 표현
  • hence it is manifest → so it's clear
  • set up → erected
  • utterly displeasing to him → completely offensive to him
  • a kind of insults → insults.
S7★★★the question that asserts + responses = oracles + heaven/earth architecture›

And is it strange that the Holy Spirit thunders such responses from heaven, when he compels even blind and miserable idolaters to make a similar confession on the earth?

🔤 Morphology · 어형
  • response < re + spondere (to pledge back) — spondere also gives sponsor, spouse, despondent; in Latin responsum is the technical term for an oracle's answer. thunder — Old English þunor, cognate with the god-name Thor and Latin tonare; the verb carries divine-utterance overtones. compel < com + pellere (to drive together) — cf. propel, expel, repel. miserable < miser (wretched) — cf. misery, commiserate.
💬 Idiom · 관용
  • Is it strange that …? = Is it any wonder that …? (= it is not); thunder responses = issue oracles with overwhelming force; compel someone to V = force someone to V; make a confession = admit something openly.
쉬운 영어 / Modern English

And is it any surprise that the Holy Spirit thunders such pronouncements from heaven, when he forces even blind and pitiful idolaters to admit the very same thing down here on earth?

Key changes · 올·현대 표현
  • is it strange that → is it any surprise that
  • responses → pronouncements, oracles
  • compels → forces
  • miserable → pitiful
  • on the earth → down here on earth.
S8★pass-through — the citation label›

Seneca’s complaint, as given by Augustine De Civit. Dei, c. 10, is well known.

🔤 Morphology · 어형
  • compel — com + pellere (drive together) → propel, expel, repel.
전체 해설 더보기

One point: is well known is a praeteritio-style move — Calvin flags the quotation as common knowledge among the educated, so his opponents cannot dismiss it as obscure. Also note the transmission chain: Calvin does not have Seneca's De Superstitione (it is lost); he has it through Augustine, and he says so. Honest sourcing. Move on. (Reference detail in Period 6.)

S9★★★Latinate OSV fronting + the subjunctive sting in the tail›

He says “The sacred immortal, and invisible gods they exhibit in the meanest and most ignoble materials, and dress them in the clothing of men and beasts; some confound the sexes, and form a compound out of different bodies, giving the name of deities to objects, which, if they were met alive, would be deemed monsters.”

🔤 Morphology · 어형
  • exhibit < ex + habere (hold out) — third habere word today. mean(est) < Old English gemǣne (common) — hence "low, base"; a trap word. ignoble < in- + gnobilis (knowable → noble) — obscure, low-born. confound < con + fundere (pour together) — cf. confuse, fusion, refund. compound < com + ponere (put together) — the same ponere as component, composite. deem — Old English dēman (to judge), cognate with doom (judgement) and deemster.
💬 Idiom · 관용
  • exhibit A in B = display A in the medium of B; dress A in the clothing of B = clothe A as B; confound the sexes = mix the genders together; form a compound out of = make a hybrid from; be deemed X = be judged to be X.
쉬운 영어 / Modern English

He says: "Those sacred, immortal, invisible gods — people display them in the cheapest and most worthless materials, and dress them in human and animal clothing. Some mix the sexes together and build hybrids out of different bodies, giving the name of gods to things that you'd call monsters if you met them alive."

Key changes · 올·현대 표현
  • the meanest and most ignoble materials → the cheapest and most worthless materials
  • confound the sexes → mix the sexes together
  • form a compound → build hybrids
  • if they were met alive → if you met them alive
  • would be deemed monsters → you'd call monsters.
S10★★★as if + past subjunctive: the evasion killed on principle›

Hence, again, it is obvious, that the defenders of images resort to a paltry quibbling evasion, when they pretend that the Jews were forbidden to use them on account of their proneness to superstition; as if a prohibition which the Lord founds on his own eternal essences and the uniform course of nature, could be restricted to a single nation.

🔤 Morphology · 어형
  • resort < Old French resortir (to go out again, fall back) — hence "fall back on." paltry — dialectal palt (rag, rubbish) — trashy, worthless. quibbling < quibble, probably from Latin quibus (the ablative plural littering legal documents) — hence "lawyer's word-play." evasion < e + vadere (to go out) — cf. invade, pervade, wade. pretend < prae + tendere (to stretch forward) — trap word: allege, not fake. proneness < pronus (leaning forward, face-down) — a built-in tendency. uniform < unus + forma — of one and the same form throughout.
💬 Idiom · 관용
  • resort to X = fall back on X (usually something inferior); a quibbling evasion = a hair-splitting dodge; pretend that = allege/claim that (archaic); on account of = because of; proneness to X = tendency toward X; as if … could = as though it were possible (implying it is not); be restricted to = be confined to.
쉬운 영어 / Modern English

So once again it's obvious that the defenders of images fall back on a feeble, hair-splitting dodge when they claim the Jews were banned from images because they were so inclined to superstition — as though a ban that the Lord bases on his own eternal being and on the unchanging order of nature could be limited to a single nation.

Key changes · 올·현대 표현
  • resort to → fall back on
  • paltry quibbling evasion → feeble, hair-splitting dodge
  • pretend that → claim that
  • proneness to superstition → tendency toward superstition
  • as if … could be restricted → as though … could be limited.
S11★★★eleven words of knockout: not A, but B›

Besides, when Paul refuted the error of giving a bodily shape to God, he was addressing not Jews, but Athenians.

🔤 Morphology · 어형
  • besides — Old English be sīdan (by the side); as an adverb, "in addition." refute < refutare (to drive back, repel) — futare is related to confute; nothing to do with "deny." address < ad + directiare (to direct toward) — to speak to, aim words at. Athenians — Ἀθηναῖοι; in Acts 17:22 Paul opens ἄνδρες Ἀθηναῖοι, "Men of Athens."
💬 Idiom · 관용
  • besides = moreover, and in addition; refute an error = drive back / disprove an error; not A, but B = not A — rather B (corrective coordination).
쉬운 영어 / Modern English

Besides, when Paul was refuting the mistake of giving God a physical form, the people he was speaking to weren't Jews — they were Athenians.

Key changes · 올·현대 표현
  • besides → what's more
  • refuted the error of → was disproving the mistake of
  • giving a bodily shape to God → giving God a physical form
  • he was addressing not Jews, but Athenians → he wasn't speaking to Jews, but to Athenians.

4 — Today's Grammar Formulas –

Formula ① — Gapping (verb ellipsis across a parallel series)

   S₁ + V + to X ;   S₂ + ∅ + to Y ;   S₃ + ∅ + to Z ;   and S₄ + ∅ + to W

   The verb appears ONCE, in the first member. Members 2–n carry it silently.
   Recognition cue: a series of ; -separated units where members 2+ have
   a subject and a prepositional phrase but NO finite main verb.

⚠️ Trap warning. In S4 each member contains a relative clause with its own is (he who **is** invisible). That is not the gapped verb. Test it: does the to-phrase make sense attached to that is? "He is invisible to a visible image" — nonsense. So carry is assimilated forward instead. Ask always: what does the to attach to?

Transformation drill. Fill the ellipsis, then rewrite in full:

The Law is fulfilled by Christ; the prophets ∅ by his suffering; the promises ∅ by his resurrection; and the covenant ∅ by his blood.

Full form: The Law is fulfilled by Christ; the prophets are fulfilled by his suffering; the promises are fulfilled by his resurrection; and the covenant is fulfilled by his blood.

Now compose one yourself, Calvin-style (four rungs, register dropping at the end):

The eternal Word is confined to a syllable; the boundless to a page; the living voice to dead ink; and the Maker of all things to a scrap of parchment.

Formula ② — as if + past subjunctive = counterfactual dismissal

   ... ; as if + S + could / were / had + ...
        = "as though it were the case that ___"
          ⤷ the speaker asserts it is NOT the case

   Companion forms:  as though + past subjunctive
                     if + S + were + ..., S + would + V   (2nd conditional, S9)

⚠️ Trap warning. The past-form modal (could, were, might) after as if is NOT past time and NOT possibility. It is the counterfactual marker. Rendering as if X could be Y as "perhaps X can be Y" inverts the author's stance from rejection to concession — and that is precisely how the wrong answer will be written.

Transformation drill. Turn the concession into a dismissal:

Given: "They say the commandment applied only to one age." Rewrite: "…as if a commandment founded on God's eternal being could be confined to one age."

Compose one yourself:

They plead that the sacraments were needed only for the unlettered — as if a sign which Christ himself instituted for his whole Church could be reserved for the ignorant.

Formula ③ — Object fronting (OSV) & the question that asserts

   (a) OBJECT-FRONTING:   [Object]  +  S  +  V  +  (adverbial)
       Cue: NP, then a subject pronoun, then the verb → the first NP is the OBJECT.
       Purpose: maximise the distance between two clashing terms.

   (b) ASSERTIVE QUESTION: Is it strange that P ?   =   It is not strange that P.
       Cue: rhetorical frame (Is it strange / Is it any wonder / Who does not see)
       Purpose: make the reader supply the conclusion.

⚠️ Trap warning. (a) If you "normalise" the word order in translation, you delete the emphasis that is the argument (S9: sacred gods … meanest materials). (b) If you translate the question as a question, you turn Calvin's assertion into hesitation. Both are meaning-changing errors, not stylistic ones.

Transformation drill.

Normal: "They display the invisible God in cheap wood." Fronted: "The invisible God they display in cheap wood."

Assertion: "It is no wonder that conscience convicts the idolater." Assertive question: "And is it strange that conscience convicts the idolater, when even his own poets condemn him?"

majesty sovereign dignity maior (greater). Roman law: laesa maiestas = treason. So insult to majesty = lèse-majesté.
⚠️ responses oracles, divine pronouncements Latin responsa = the technical word for an oracle's answer at Delphi. Not "replies."

⚠️ mean(est) lowest, basest OE gemǣne = common → base. Not "cruel." the meanest materials = the cheapest stuff.
ignoble low-born, of no standing in- + gnobilis (knowable → noble). Un-know-worthy.
⚠️ confound mix together, pour together con + fundere → confuse, fusion, refund. Not "puzzle."
compound (n.) a hybrid, thing put together com + ponere → component, composite, compost.
deem judge to be OE dēman → doom (judgement), deemster. To deem is to doom.

evasion a dodge e + vadere (go out) → invade, pervade, wade.
⚠️ pretend allege, claim, put forward as pretext prae + tendere (stretch forward) → pretext, pretension. Not "make believe."

uniform of one form throughout unus + forma. Not "a costume" — unvarying.

6 — Background in 5 Minutes –

The doctrine at stake. This is the Second Commandment as a live, binding, universal law — and everything in Reformed worship follows from how you answer it. Calvin's grounding is not aesthetic and not pastoral. It is ontological: God is spirit, infinite, invisible; any image therefore asserts something false about him. That is why the argument survives the "but the Jews were superstitious" objection. A ban grounded in what God is cannot expire when a culture matures.

The numbering fight. Note what Rome and Luther did with this text: they fold the image prohibition into the First Commandment and split coveting into two to keep the total at ten. Calvin (with the Eastern church and the Reformed tradition) keeps "no graven image" as a separate Second Commandment. This is not bookkeeping. A separate commandment is a separate, standing obligation; an appendix to the first can be read as a specification of idolatry-in-general and therefore as satisfied whenever you don't literally worship the statue. Chapter 11 exists to prevent exactly that move.

Seneca via Augustine — get the citation right. Seneca wrote De Superstitione, and it is lost. Everything we have comes through Augustine, who quotes it in City of God Book VI, chapter 10 — Beveridge prints De Civit. Dei, c. 10 without the book number, which is why students hunt for it in the wrong place. Augustine's point there is that Seneca, as a Stoic, despised popular religion privately while conforming publicly (Augustine's cutting line: he worshipped what he censured). Calvin uses the passage for a narrower purpose: not to praise Seneca, but to show the truth is so obvious that even a pagan Stoic sees it and cannot un-see it. That is the inexcusability argument from I.3 and I.5, running on a new example.

The lineage. Behind Calvin stand the pre-Constantinian fathers he will name in §6 — Lactantius, Eusebius, the Council of Elvira's canon 36 — and behind them the whole aniconic strand of the early church. Ahead of him lies the great fault line of the Reformation's second decade: Karlstadt at Wittenberg (1522) tearing images out immediately; Luther returning from the Wartburg to stop him, insisting images are adiaphora — indifferent things — and that only the heart's idolatry matters; Zwingli stripping Zurich's churches in 1524. Calvin lands between Karlstadt's violence and Luther's tolerance: images of God are always forbidden, but the removal is the magistrate's work, done in order, not the mob's.

Later reception. The 1546 Council of Trent (Session 25) answered this chapter directly, permitting images while forbidding that "any divinity or virtue" be thought to reside in them. In the twentieth century Barth made Calvin's principle the engine of the whole Church Dogmatics prolegomena — God is known only where he gives himself to be known, never in a form we construct — and it is the intellectual parent of the Regulative Principle of Worship in the Reformed confessions.

⚠️ Over-reading guardrail — three limits, and every one of them gets breached by careless readers.

  1. Calvin is not against art. At I.11.12 he says plainly that sculpture and painting are gifts of God, and that histories and figures of things that can be seen may lawfully be represented. His target is the representation of God and the worship of images, not visual culture.
  2. This is not an argument against materiality. Calvin is not a Platonist fleeing the body — remember Chapter 5, where the whole visible universe is the theatre of God's glory. The problem with the wooden god is not that it is matter; it is that it is matter claiming to be a likeness of the invisible.
  3. He does not concede the Jews were uniquely prone to superstition. He does not fight that claim at all — he makes it irrelevant by shifting to the ban's ground. Do not report Calvin as agreeing with the premise. He simply refuses to let it bear any weight.

7 — Scripture Connections –

  1. Deut. 4:15–16 (quoted in S2) — "ye saw no manner of similitude." Hebrew תְּמוּנָה (temunah) = form, shape, likeness — deliberately the same word as Exod. 20:4, which Calvin quoted yesterday in §1. So S2 is not a new proof text; it is Moses supplying the reason for the text already cited. That is precisely what annexes in S1 promised. How Calvin uses it: the revelation at Horeb was auditory (the Lord spake) inside concealment (the midst of the fire) — form withheld on purpose.

  2. Isaiah 40:18 (cited in S4) — "To whom then will ye liken God? or what likeness (דְּמוּת demuth) will ye compare unto him?" The rhetorical question with no possible answer. Calvin's four-rung ladder in S4 is essentially this verse expanded into a syllogism.

  3. Isaiah 41:7 (S4) — the craftsmen encouraging one another, then "he fastened it with nails, that it should not be moved." The god who needs carpentry to stay upright. Isaiah's mockery; Calvin's paltry wood.

  4. Isaiah 41:29 (S4) — "their molten images are wind and confusion." Hebrew תֹּהוּ (tohu) — the same tohu as Genesis 1:2's formlessness. The image-maker produces anti-creation.

  5. Isaiah 45:9 (S4) — "Woe unto him that striveth with his Maker!" The potter/clay reversal: the shaped thing shaping its shaper. This is the theological engine under the whole chapter.

  6. Isaiah 46:5 (S4) — "To whom will ye liken me, and make me equal, and compare me, that we may be like?" Four verbs of likening in one verse, all refused.

  7. Acts 17:29 (quoted in S5, and the hinge of S11) — γένος … τοῦ θεοῦ (offspring/kin of God), and τὸ θεῖον for "the Godhead." Calvin's masterstroke is in the second word: τὸ θεῖον is the Greek philosophers' own abstraction for "the divine." Paul takes their vocabulary and uses it against their statuary. And the medium is doubly indicted — χαράγματι τέχνης καὶ ἐνθυμήσεως ἀνθρώπου, "by the engraving of human craft and human imagination": hand and mind, both merely human. How Calvin uses it: S11 turns the audience of this verse into the refutation of the "Jews only" evasion.

  8. Exod. 20:4 (echoed in S3, all figures; S6, whatever statues … or pictures) — carried over from yesterday. The universal quantifiers all and whatever are Calvin re-asserting §1's without exception.

  9. Rom. 1:23 (unnamed, standing behind S6 and S9) — "changed the glory of the uncorruptible God into an image made like to corruptible man, and to birds, and fourfooted beasts, and creeping things." Seneca's "dress them in the clothing of men and beasts" is startlingly close, and Calvin surely enjoys the parallel: the Stoic independently reproduces Paul's indictment. That coincidence is the argument of S7 — earth confessing what heaven thunders.

  10. Isaiah 44:9–20 (the deep background to S4 and S9) — the carpenter who burns half the log for warmth, bakes bread over it, and worships the remainder. Beveridge's a bit of paltry wood is a compressed allusion to this passage.

8 — Exam Problems –

① Grammar — one underline is wrong. Find it.

Of the prophets, it will be sufficient to mention Isaiah, in order to show how the majesty of God ⓐ is defiled by an absurd fiction, when he who is incorporeal ⓑ is assimilated to corporeal matter; he who is invisible ⓒ is invisible to a visible image; and he who fills all space ⓓ to a bit of paltry wood.

Answer: ⓒ

Rationale (exam-setter's note). ⓒ is the gapping trap, and it is built to look more grammatical than the correct reading. In the source, member 2 is he who is invisible **to** a visible image — with no finite main verb, because is assimilated is carried forward from member 1. The distractor "restores" a verb by duplicating is invisible, which produces a semantic absurdity ("he is invisible to a visible image") while looking tidy. ⓐ and ⓑ are the correct passives; ⓓ correctly shows the bare to-phrase of a fully gapped member. The diagnostic question is always: what does the to-phrase attach to?

② Comprehension — which is correct?

  1. Calvin concedes that the prohibition of images was given because Israel was unusually inclined to superstition, but argues that Christians should still observe it out of prudence.
  2. Calvin cites Seneca to show that the truth about images is so evident that God extracts the confession even from idolaters themselves.
  3. Calvin allows that a representation in the human form, being nobler than wood or stone, is less offensive to God's majesty.
  4. Calvin argues that because the Holy Spirit speaks from heaven, testimony from earthly and pagan sources is worthless in this matter.

Answer: 2

Rationale. - 1 — direction-flip. Calvin calls that argument a paltry quibbling evasion (S10). He does not concede it and then rescue the command on prudential grounds; he denies that the command was ever grounded there. This is the section's most seductive wrong answer. - 2 — correct. S7 (he **compels** even blind and miserable idolaters) + S8–S9. The verb compels is the whole point: it is extracted testimony, not agreement. - 3 — scope-overreach, imported from §1. Yesterday's S10 explicitly forbids it: God makes no comparison between images. Today's S4 lists gold in the same breath as wood and stone. No gradations exist anywhere in this chapter. - 4 — inverted logic. S7's structure is heaven and earth in agreement (from heaven … on the earth). The pagan testimony is not dismissed; it is the reason there is nowhere left to appeal.

③ Composition — apply today's formulas.

Translate into Calvin-register English, using (i) as if + past subjunctive for the dismissal and (ii) gapping for the parallel series.

그들은 이 금지가 옛 시대에만 해당한다고 주장한다 — 마치 하나님 자신의 영원한 본질에 근거한 법이 한 시대에 갇힐 수 있다는 듯이. 그러나 무한하신 분이 한 뼘의 나무에, 보이지 않으신 분이 그려진 형상에, 살아계신 분이 죽은 돌에 견주어진다.

Model answer:

They pretend that this prohibition belonged to a former age only; as if a law which the Lord founds on his own eternal essence could be shut up within a single age. But he who is infinite is assimilated to a span of wood; he who is invisible ∅ to a painted figure; and he who is living ∅ to dead stone.

Rationale. Three things are being marked. (i) as if … could — the past-form modal, not "may" or "might"; the frame must reject, not concede. (ii) The verb is assimilated appears once; members 2 and 3 carry only subject + to-phrase. If you wrote it out three times you understood the meaning but not the structure. (iii) Bonus credit for pretend that in the archaic sense (= allege) and for the register-drop a span of wood mirroring Calvin's a bit of paltry wood.

9 — One-Line Wrap-up + Homework –

One-line summary. The image-ban is not arbitrary and not national: Moses, Isaiah, and Paul each supply the reason God annexed to it — that the incorporeal, invisible, living, all-filling God is falsified by every material likeness — and since that reason rests on God's own eternal essence and the uniform course of nature, the "it was only for superstition-prone Jews" evasion collapses, refuted on principle (S10) and by the plain fact that Paul preached it to Athenians (S11); the three structures that carried it were gapping (S4, S6), as if + past subjunctive (S10), and object-fronting / the assertive question (S9, S7).

Homework (10 minutes).

  1. Structure restoration. Take S9 and rewrite it in plain SVO order — restore they exhibit the sacred, immortal, and invisible gods…. Then read both aloud. Write one sentence on exactly what is lost when the fronting disappears. (Hint: measure the distance between sacred and meanest.)
  2. Composition. Write one sentence in English using Is it strange that … ? as an assertive question, with a when-clause supplying the reason, on this theme: conscience convicting a man who denies God's existence. Then rewrite it as a flat declarative and note which version is harder to argue with.
  3. Preview — §3 (Day 58). Tomorrow Calvin turns and faces the strongest counter-evidence in the Bible. It opens: "It is true that the Lord occasionally manifested his presence by certain signs, so that he was said to be seen face to face…" — the cloud, the fire, the dove, the mercy-seat, and the Cherubim. If God showed himself in visible signs, why can we not make visible signs? Watch for the answer already coded into the first sentence: **in apt accordance with the scheme of doctrine** — and note that Calvin will argue the Cherubim were placed behind a veil, in the dark, precisely so that no one could look at them.
