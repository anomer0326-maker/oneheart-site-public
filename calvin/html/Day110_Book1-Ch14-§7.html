Day 110 — Book 1, Chapter 14, §7

0 — Orientation (1 minute) –

Yesterday Calvin said angels are God's delegated protectors. Today a student raises a hand: "Does each of us have one of our own?" Look at how Calvin answers. Not "yes." Not "no." He says: "I dare not positively affirm." That is a theologian declining to go beyond the text — and then he tells you exactly what he does hold for certain: not one angel, but all of them, with one consent, watch for our safety. Underline that. The whole section is a lesson in disciplined agnosticism, wrapped around a corporate doctrine of angelic care.

Why examiners love it. Fifteen sentences, five Bible texts (Daniel, Matthew, Luke ×2, 2 Kings), one hard counter-text (Peter at the door, Acts 12), and a two-step argument that is built to be misread: Calvin says the evidence does not prove a single guardian — he does not say it disproves one. Direction-flip heaven. And the grammar is rich: a fronted Whether or not … noun clause as the object of affirm (S1), a fronted This + I hold for certain, that … with a not … merely, but … correlative (S5), a monster sentence with nothing to prevent us from … without implying that … and a dangling that-clause (S12), and a who … limit … do great injury subject–verb gap (S15).

Text traps. ⚠️ intimates (S2) = hints, suggests — not "threatens." ⚠️ insinuates (S3) = suggests indirectly — neutral here, not "sneaks in a slur." ⚠️ presidents (S2) = those who preside over, governors — not heads of state. ⚠️ with one consent (S5) = unanimously, in agreement (consent < con- + sentire, feel together). ⚠️ genii (S12) = plural of genius in the old sense: a guardian spirit attached to a person. ⚠️ auxiliary troops (S15) = reinforcements.

Today's 3 Big Points

  1. Fronted Whether or not noun clause as the object of affirm: Whether or not each believer has a single angel assigned to him for his defence, I dare not positively affirm (S1) → Restore: I dare not positively affirm whether or not each believer has… dare not = a modal-like negation (no -s, no do). Formula 1.
  2. Fronted This + correlative not … merely, but …: This, indeed, I hold for certain, that each of us is cared for, not by one angel merely, but that all with one consent watch for our safety (S5) → This is the object of hold; the that-clause is in apposition to it. Match the parallel: not by one angel … but [that] all… Formula 2.
  3. nothing to prevent us from V-ing … without implying that … and Those … who limit … do great injury (S12, S15) → prevent X from V-ing is the frame; without implying that governs a clause with its own according to insertion. In S15 find the main verb before you read the relative clauses. Formula 3.

1 — Full Text (⚠️ invariable) — 15 sentences –

Whether or not each believer has a single angel assigned to him for his defence, I dare not positively affirm. When Daniel introduces the angel of the Persian and the angel of the Greeks, he undoubtedly intimates that certain angels are appointed as a kind of presidents over kingdoms and provinces. Again, when Christ says that the angels of children always behold the face of his Father, he insinuates that there are certain angels to whom their safety has been entrusted. But I know not if it can be inferred from this, that each believer has his own angel. This, indeed, I hold for certain, that each of us is cared for, not by one angel merely, but that all with one consent watch for our safety. For it is said of all the angels collectively, that they rejoice “over one sinner that repenteth, more than over ninety and nine just persons which need no repentance.” It is also said, that the angels (meaning more than one) carried the soul of Lazarus into Abraham’s bosom. Nor was it to no purpose that Elisha showed his servant the many chariots of fire which were specially allotted him. There is one passage which seems to intimate somewhat more clearly that each individual has a separate angel. When Peter, after his deliverance from prison, knocked at the door of the house where the brethren were assembled, being unable to think it could be himself, they said that it was his angel. This idea seems to have been suggested to them by a common belief that every believer has a single angel assigned to him. Here, however, it may be alleged, that there is nothing to prevent us from understanding it of any one of the angels to whom the Lord might have given the charge of Peter at that particular time, without implying that he was to be his, perpetual guardian, according to the vulgar imagination (see Calvin on Mark 5:9), that two angels a good and a bad, as a kind of genii, are assigned to each individual. After all, it is not worthwhile anxiously to investigate a point which does not greatly concern us. If any one does not think it enough to know that all the orders of the heavenly host are perpetually watching for his safety, I do not see what he could gain by knowing that he has one angel as a special guardian. Those, again, who limit the care which God takes of each of us to a single angel, do great injury to themselves and to all the members of the Church, as if there were no value in those promises of auxiliary troops, who on every side encircling and defending us, embolden us to fight more manfully.

2 — Structure at a Glance – 

[QUESTION]      S1       Does each believer have ONE assigned angel?  Calvin: "I dare not positively affirm."
[WHAT'S SHOWN]  S2–S3    Scripture shows angels over kingdoms (Daniel) and over "little ones" (Christ) — group-level assignment
[CAUTION]       S4       But that does not PROVE a personal angel for each believer
[CERTAIN]       S5–S8    What I hold for certain: each of us is cared for by ALL the angels (joy over one sinner; Lazarus; Elisha's chariots)
[HARD CASE]     S9–S12   Peter at the door: "It is his angel" — popular belief; the text does not oblige us (and the 'two genii' idea is vulgar)
[VERDICT]       S13–S15  Not worth anxious inquiry; the corporate promise is enough; limiting it to ONE angel IMPOVERISHES us

Examiner's Eye. - ⚠️ Direction-flip (denial vs. suspension): Calvin does not deny a single guardian angel. S1: "I dare not positively affirm"; S4: "I know not if it can be inferred." An option saying "Calvin rejects the idea of a guardian angel" is wrong. He suspends judgment and calls the question unprofitable (S13). - ⚠️ Scope overreach (what is "certain"): The certain point (S5) is that all the angels care for each of us. It is not "no angel is ever specially assigned" (S8 even says the chariots were specially allotted to Elisha). - ⚠️ Detail trap (Acts 12): In S10 it is the brethren ("they") who say "it is his angel," because they were unable to think it could be Peter himself. Calvin treats this as their belief, not as an apostolic teaching. - ⚠️ Detail trap (S12 "two angels"): The "good and bad angel" idea is called "vulgar imagination" — Calvin rejects it. Do not confuse it with the single guardian question. - ⚠️ Scope overreach (S15): The harm Calvin names is to those who limit God's care to a single angel — not to those who hold that angels exist or protect.

4 — Today's Grammar Formulas –

Formula 1 — Fronted whether (or not) noun clause as object + modal-like dare not (S1)

[Whether or not S V …], S dare not V.        ←   S dare not V [whether or not S V …].
Whether or not each believer has a single angel, I dare not positively affirm.
   =  I dare not positively affirm whether or not each believer has a single angel.

⚠️ Trap. A fronted object clause leaves the main clause without an object — that gap is the clue. Also, dare not takes a bare infinitive (affirm), no to, and no do. Do not read whether or not as a concessive "no matter whether" adverbial here. Drill. I do not dare to say whether the Lord appoints one angel to each child. → Whether the Lord appoints one angel to each child, I dare not say.

Formula 2 — Fronted This + hold for certain, that … + correlative not … merely, but … (S5)

This, (indeed,) S hold for certain, [that S1 is V-ed, not by A merely, but that S2 V …]
   =  S hold for certain that S1 is V-ed not merely by A but by B.

⚠️ Trap. This is the object of hold; the that-clause fills it (apposition). The second member of the correlative (but that all … watch) is a second that-clause, parallel to the first, not a phrase parallel to by one angel. Memory hook: not … merely, but … = not only … but also …. Drill. Calvin is sure of one thing: God defends us through all his angels, not through one only. → This Calvin holds for certain, that God defends us, not through one angel merely, but that all his angels with one consent watch for our safety.

Formula 3 — nothing to prevent us from V-ing … without implying that … + a long subject with an inserted relative (S12, S15)

There is nothing to prevent us from V-ing O (of X), without implying that S V …
Those, again, who V …, do great injury to …, as if there were no value in …

⚠️ Trap. (a) In S12, without implying that … hangs on understanding; it is a negative gloss ("while not meaning that…"). (b) In S15 the subject is long (Those … who limit … to a single angel) and the main verb do comes late — find it before analysing the insertions. (c) as if there were no value = counterfactual subjunctive. Drill. Nothing stops us from calling Scripture sufficient. This does not mean that we despise the Church's teaching. → There is nothing to prevent us from calling Scripture sufficient, without implying that we despise the Church's teaching.

6 — Background in 5 Minutes –

The doctrine at stake. The guardian angel question: does each person have a personal angel assigned? This is a case study in Calvin's method: (1) lay out the Scripture evidence (Daniel, Mt 18:10, Lk 15:7, Lk 16:22, 2 Kgs 6:17, Acts 12:15); (2) say plainly what it proves (angels are assigned to groups; all angels care for each believer); (3) say plainly what it doesn't prove (a single lifelong angel for each); (4) declare the leftover question unprofitable. This is exactly the discipline he set in §4's two rules.

The pastoral reason. S15's sting is not that individual guardianship is false, but that making it the rule shrinks the promises. The Psalms speak of an encamping host (Ps 34:7), of angels "all" being ministering spirits (Heb 1:14). A single assigned angel — however comforting — understates what the text offers.

The Acts 12 hinge (S9–S12). This is the strongest counter-text and Calvin handles it with the three-way move: (a) report it; (b) note that it is the brethren's statement and that it reflects a popular belief (S11); (c) show that the text can be read without the lifelong-guardian idea (S12). He doesn't say the brethren were wrong in believing in angels — only that Luke's reporting doesn't endorse the individual-assignment doctrine.

Lineage. The idea that each person has a personal angel has a long history: popular in Greco-Roman religion (the genius, the daimōn), found in early Christian writing (the Shepherd of Hermas speaks of two angels attending a person), and often drawn from Mt 18:10 in the Fathers (Jerome is commonly cited). Aquinas holds that each person has a guardian angel (ST I, q. 113, a. 2), and it is a settled teaching in later Roman Catholic piety. Calvin's position — I dare not positively affirm — sits between assertion and denial. Later Reformed writers varied; some accepted individual guardianship as probable, others stayed with the corporate doctrine. Be careful: this is a general sketch of the tradition, not a claim about every author.

Guardrails against over-reading. (1) Calvin does not deny personal guardian angels; he suspends judgment (S1, S4) and calls the inquiry unprofitable (S13). (2) The "certain" point (S5) is corporate care; it is not a denial that an angel may be specially charged for a time (S8, S12). (3) The "two angels, good and bad" idea (S12) is rejected as vulgar imagination; do not read it as Calvin's own. (4) Nothing here licenses prayer to angels; the whole chapter keeps God as the one who undertakes protection (see §6, S5).

7 — Scripture Connections –

  1. S1–S4 — Dan 10:13, 20–21; 12:1. The "prince of Persia," "prince of Grecia," and Michael "the great prince which standeth for the children of thy people." Calvin reads these as angels set over realms.
  2. S3 — Mt 18:10. "Take heed that ye despise not one of these little ones; for I say unto you, That in heaven their angels do always behold the face of my Father which is in heaven." (The source's footnote gives 18:20, a slip.) Calvin: certain angels are entrusted with children's safety — not necessarily one each.
  3. S6 — Lk 15:7, 10. "Joy shall be in heaven over one sinner that repenteth, more than over ninety and nine just persons, which need no repentance" / "joy in the presence of the angels of God over one sinner that repenteth." Plural angels, one sinner.
  4. S7 — Lk 16:22. "…the beggar died, and was carried by the angels into Abraham's bosom." Plural angels, one soul.
  5. S8 — 2 Kgs 6:15–17. The young man sees "the mountain … full of horses and chariots of fire round about Elisha." Calvin: many chariots, specially allotted to one man.
  6. S9–S11 — Acts 12:12–16. Peter at the door of the house of Mary; the maid Rhoda; "Thou art mad … Then said they, It is his angel." (Gk ho angelos autou estin). Calvin: a statement of the brethren's belief, recorded by Luke.
  7. S12 — Mk 5:9. The reference "see Calvin on Mark 5:9" (the legion of demons) is Calvin's own cross-reference in the Harmony commentary to the notion of two spirits attending each person.
  8. S14–S15 — Ps 34:7; Heb 1:14. "The angel of the Lord encampeth round about them that fear him" and "ministering spirits, sent forth to minister for them who shall be heirs of salvation" — the auxiliary troops of S15.

How Calvin uses Scripture: he reads the texts for what they say, not for what tradition adds. When a text could carry a stronger doctrine (Acts 12), he shows the weaker reading is available and refuses to build on it. Contrast the pseudo-Dionysian method in §4: speculation from texts vs. restraint under texts.

8 — Exam Problems (출제자의 눈) –

① Grammar — one underlined part is wrong. Which?

Calvin says (A) Whether or not each believer has a single angel assigned to him, I dare not positively affirm; (B) this, indeed, I hold for certain, that each of us is cared for, not by one angel merely, but that all with one consent watches for our safety; (C) there is nothing to prevent us from understanding it of any one of the angels; and (D) those who limit the care to a single angel do great injury to themselves.

Answer: (B). Rationale: the subject of the second that-clause is all (plural), so the verb must be watch, not watches (Formula 2). (A) is the S1 fronted noun clause with dare not + bare infinitive (Formula 1). (C) is correct: prevent X from V-ing (Formula 3). (D) is correct: the main verb do agrees with Those (plural), not with a single angel (Formula 3).

② Comprehension — which statement is consistent with §7?

(A) Calvin argues that Scripture proves each believer has one permanent guardian angel, as shown by Acts 12. (B) Calvin denies that there are any angels assigned to kingdoms or to children. (C) Calvin declines to affirm that each believer has a single assigned angel, but holds for certain that all the angels together watch over each of us, and thinks it unprofitable to press the question further. (D) Calvin holds that every person is assigned two angels, one good and one bad, as a kind of genii.

Answer: (C). Rationale: (A) direction-flip — S1/S4/S12: he refuses to affirm and offers a reading of Acts 12 that does not require it. (B) direction-flip — S2–S3: Daniel and Christ's words intimate that certain angels are appointed over kingdoms and over children. (D) direction-flip — S12 calls this the vulgar imagination. Correct answer (C) combines S1 (decline), S5 (certain: all angels), and S13 (not worth anxious inquiry).

③ Composition — use Formula 1 and Formula 3.

Compose one English sentence (≈25 words): I do not dare to assert whether each church has a single guardian angel. Nothing stops us from reading the text of a temporary charge. This does not imply a permanent assignment.

Examiner's intent: fronted whether or not object clause + dare not + nothing to prevent us from V-ing + without implying. Alternative accepted (Formula 2): This I hold for certain, that the church is cared for, not by one angel merely, but that all with one consent watch for its safety.

9 — One-Line Wrap-up + Homework –

One-line summary: Calvin dares not positively affirm that each believer has a single assigned angel — Daniel and Christ show angels assigned to groups, Acts 12 only records the brethren's popular belief, and the "two genii" idea is vulgar — but he holds for certain that all the angels with one consent watch over each of us, calls the further question unprofitable, and warns that confining God's care to one angel impoverishes the promises of the heavenly host; the grammar keys are the fronted Whether or not object clause with dare not (S1), the fronted This with not … merely, but … (S5), and nothing to prevent us from V-ing … without implying that … with the long-subject Those … who … do (S12, S15).
