Day 96 — Book 1, Chapter 13, §22

0 — Orientation (1 minute) –

Yesterday ended with order: Father first, the Son from him, the Spirit from both. Today someone in the back row puts up a hand: "Professor — if the Son is FROM the Father, isn't he less? And if there are three, isn't God split?" Calvin's first sentence answers with a construction you must own: this distinction is so far from interfering with the most perfect unity of God, that the Son may thereby be proved to be one God with the Father. ⚠️⚠️ so far from V-ing … that … = far from doing X, it actually does the opposite. The distinction doesn't damage the unity. It is the evidence for it. Formula 1.

Second — the key word of the whole section is relation. Augustine: By those names which denote distinctions … is meant the relation which they mutually bear to each other, not the very substance by which they are one. ⚠️ Look at the word order: a prepositional phrase first, then the verb is meant, and only THEN the real subject the relation. Students grab those names as the subject and write are meant. Wrong. Formula 2.

Third — the most famous theological move in Chapter 13 is born here. The Son is God of himself — autotheos — and yet the Father is the beginning of the Son. Contradiction? No: as to himself vs as to the Father. ⚠️⚠️⚠️ as to here is not concerning (a topic). It is in relation to / considered with respect to — Latin ad se, ad Patrem. Two viewpoints, one God. And then the brake again: it is far safer to rest contented … than get bewildered — than + a bare infinitive. Formula 3.

Today's 3 Big Points

  1. so far from V-ing … that … (paradox of result) + two parallel that-clauses + inasmuch as (= because). ⚠️ The first that is the result clause of so far from; the second and that is its twin, sharing the same frame. inasmuch as = since, because — NOT to the extent that. (S1.) Formula 1.
  2. Word-order machinery: fronted PP + inversion (By those names … is meant the relation), negative inversion (nor do ecclesiastical writers admit), gapping (the whole Son ‹is› in the Father), and the absolute clause (the only difference being that …). ⚠️ Find the real subject after the verb; nor pulls the auxiliary in front; the absolute clause has no finite verb. (S2, S3, S4.) Formula 2.
  3. The relational qualifier as to himself … as to the Father + balanced when … simply …; but when … relation … + it is far safer to V than ‹to› V. ⚠️ as to X = with respect to X; simply = absolutely, without qualification (not merely); after than in an infinitive comparison, to may drop. (S7–S10, S12.) Formula 3.

1 — Full Text (⚠️ invariable) — 12 sentences –

Moreover, this distinction is so far from interfering with the most perfect unity of God, that the Son may thereby be proved to be one God with the Father, inasmuch as he constitutes one Spirit with him, and that the Spirit is not different from the Father and the Son, inasmuch as he is the Spirit of the Father and the Son. In each hypostasis the whole nature is understood the only difference being that each has his own peculiar subsistence. The whole Father is in the Son, and the whole Son in the Father, as the Son himself also declares (John 14:10), “I am in the Father, and the Father in me;” nor do ecclesiastical writers admit that the one is separated from the other by any difference of essence. “By those names which denote distinctions” says Augustine “is meant the relation which they mutually bear to each other, not the very substance by which they are one.” In this way, the sentiments of the Fathers, which might sometimes appear to be at variance with each other, are to be reconciled. At one time they teach that the Father is the beginning of the Son, at another they assert that the Son has both divinity and essence from himself, and therefore is one beginning with the Father. The cause of this discrepancy is well and clearly explained by Augustine, when he says, “Christ, as to himself, is called God, as to the Father he is called Son.” And again, “The Father, as to himself, is called God, as to the Son he is called Father. He who, as to the Son, is called Father, is not Son; and he who, as to himself, is called Father, and he who, as to himself, is called Son, is the same God.” Therefore, when we speak of the Son simply, without reference to the Father, we truly and properly affirm that he is of himself, and, accordingly, call him the only beginning; but when we denote the relation which he bears to the Father, we correctly make the Father the beginning of the Son. Augustine’s fifth book on the Trinity is wholly devoted to the explanation of this subject. But it is far safer to rest contented with the relation as taught by him, than get bewildered in vain speculation by subtle prying into a sublime mystery.

Sentence count: 12 (S1–S12). One paragraph, four movements: the thesis — distinction proves unity (S1–S3) · the key — names signify relation, not substance (S4) · the fathers reconciled by Augustine's "as to" (S5–S9) · Calvin's rule and the brake (S10–S12).

[RULE]           S10   Son SIMPLY → OF HIMSELF, the only beginning

The spine in one line: Distinction doesn't split God — it proves the Son and Spirit are the one God → each Person has the whole nature; they differ only by their own subsistence → so the names name relations, not substances → so "the Father is the Son's beginning" and "the Son is God of himself" are both true — from two angles → say that, and stop.

Examiner's Eye. ⚠️⚠️⚠️ Five traps. (1) S1 — direction. ⚠️ The distinction does NOT threaten the unity; it proves it. An option saying Calvin concedes that the distinction weakens the unity but accepts it is a direction flip. (2) S1 — one Spirit. ⚠️⚠️ he constitutes one Spirit with him = the Son shares with the Father the one spiritual divine being (God is a Spirit, John 4:24). It is NOT a statement about the Holy Spirit as the third Person. Options that read it as the Son and Father together form the Holy Spirit are nonsense traps. (3) S2 — how much of the nature is in each Person? ⚠️ the whole. Not a third each. An option saying each Person possesses a part of the divine nature flips S2 (and S3: The whole Father … the whole Son). (4) S6–S10 — does Calvin pick one side of the fathers? ⚠️⚠️ No. He reconciles (S5). Both statements are kept: the Father is the beginning of the Son (as to relation) AND the Son is of himself (as to essence / considered simply). An option saying Calvin rejects the view that the Father is the beginning of the Son is a half-truth trap — scope overreach. (5) S12 — does Calvin dismiss Augustine's Book V? ⚠️ No — he commends it (S11) and says rest contented with the relation as taught by him. What he rejects is vain speculation and subtle prying beyond the relation. And him = Augustine, not Christ.

6 — Background in 5 Minutes –

Where we are. Chapter 13's second block (our §§20–23 = printed §§17–20): distinction (Day 94) → order (Day 95) → today, relation: why the distinction and order do NOT split the One → tomorrow (printed §20), the summary for "those who love soberness". Today is the hinge between the positive doctrine and the polemics that close the chapter (printed §§21–29 against Servetus, Gentile and the anti-trinitarians).

Augustine's key: substance vs relation. The Arians (Eunomius especially) argued: God is unbegotten; the Son is begotten; unbegotten ≠ begotten; so they differ in substance. Augustine (De Trinitate V) answered with a distinction from Aristotle's categories — but corrected for God: in God there are no accidents; yet not everything said of God is said by substance. Some things are said ad se (of God in himself: God, good, great, eternal) — one for all three. Some are said ad aliquid (relatively: Father of…, Son of…) — these distinguish without dividing. S4 quotes that principle; S11 points to Book V. The Cappadocians had reached the same point from the Greek side (Gregory of Nazianzus: Father is the name of a relation, not of an essence or an activity — Oration 29.16).

Beveridge's note 46 — the sources. The note cites Augustine's Homilies (De Temp. 38), De Trinitate, Letter 174 to Pascentius, Cyril of Alexandria's De Trinitate (Dialogues, book 7) and his third Dialogue, Augustine on Psalms 59 and 68 and Tractates on John 89. The "as to himself … as to the Father" formula is usually located in Augustine's Expositions of the Psalms (on Ps 68). Treat the exact numbering of these older citations with caution — Beveridge reproduces 16th-century reference styles.

The autotheos move (S6, S10) — Calvin's signature. The Nicene Creed confesses the Son as God from God (Deum de Deo). Calvin affirms that — as to the Person (S10b: we correctly make the Father the beginning of the Son). But as to the essence, the Son's deity is not a derived, lesser deity handed down: the Son is God of himself (S10a). Why press it? Because Gentile and others argued: only the Father is autotheos; the Son has an essence given to him — making the Son a secondary God. Calvin's answer (printed §§23–25): the essence is not begotten; the Person is. Reception: Roman critics (e.g. Gilbert Génébrard) charged Calvin with innovation; Robert Bellarmine (De Christo) examined the question and is often cited as judging that Calvin's meaning could be taken in a sound sense, though he disliked the wording. B. B. Warfield ("Calvin's Doctrine of the Trinity", 1909) hailed autotheotes as Calvin's chief contribution to the doctrine. Reformed orthodoxy generally followed Calvin: the Son is autotheos as to essence, from the Father as to person.

Perichoresis (S3). John 14:10 underwrites the doctrine of mutual indwelling — later named περιχώρησις by John of Damascus (8th c.), circumincessio in the Latin West. Calvin doesn't use the technical term; he uses the verse and the mirror sentence.

Over-reading guardrails, all in one place. ⚠️ (1) Relation ≠ mere names or roles (modalism): the relations are real — they are the Persons' peculiar subsistence (S2). (2) autotheos ≠ denial of eternal generation: Calvin keeps the Father the beginning of the Son (S10b). (3) one Spirit in S1 = one spiritual divine essence (John 4:24), not the Holy Spirit. (4) the only beginning (S10) ≠ the Son excluding the Father — as God, the three are one principle (S6: one beginning with the Father). (5) S12 ≠ anti-intellectualism: Calvin recommends Augustine's Book V (S11); he forbids speculation beyond the revealed relation. (6) beginning ≠ temporal start (Day 95 S6: no room for first or last).

The one-line takeaway. ⚠️ Same God in himself; Father and Son in relation — say both, and stop there.

9 — One-Line Wrap-up + Homework –

One-line summary. this distinction is so far from interfering with the most perfect unity of God, that the Son may thereby be proved to be one God with the Father* — In each hypostasis the whole nature is understood, the only difference being that each has his own peculiar subsistence; The whole Father is in the Son, and the whole Son in the Father (John 14:10); the names mean the relation which they mutually bear to each other, not the very substance by which they are one (Augustine) — so the fathers are to be reconciled: Christ, as to himself, is called God, as to the Father he is called Son; when we speak of the Son simply … he is of himself, but when we denote the relation … we correctly make the Father the beginning of the Son — and it is far safer to rest contented with the relation … than get bewildered in vain speculation*.

The three grammar points, once more. ① so far from V-ing … that …, and that … + inasmuch as = because (S1). ② Word-order machinery — By those names … is meant the relation (S4: real subject after the verb), nor do ecclesiastical writers admit (S3), the only difference being that (S2: absolute), the whole Son ‹is› in the Father (S3: gapping). ③ Relational qualifier + rule + brake — as to himself / as to the Father (S7–S9), when … simply …; but when … relation … (S10), far safer to rest … than get bewildered (S12).
